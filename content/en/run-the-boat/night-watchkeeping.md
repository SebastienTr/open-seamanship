A collision almost always comes down to the same thing: someone was not
watching, or did not understand what they saw. A French accident study of 76
collisions between fishing boats and merchant ships found that a failure of
lookout and of judgement by one or both vessels was nearly always the
deciding factor
([BEAmer 2001, introduction](../library/beamer-abordages-peche-commerce-2001.html)).
The rules of the road require a proper lookout at all times, by sight and
hearing ([MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 26](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html))
and by every other means suited to the conditions, such as radar. Good
visibility does not excuse you from using the radar
([BEAmer 2001, section 10](../library/beamer-abordages-peche-commerce-2001.html)).
For who gives way, see [Rules of the road](rules-of-the-road.html).

## What the person on watch does

The watchkeeper reports anything that may matter to the boat's navigation:
ships, land, lights, buoys, discoloured water, breakers, fog signals. Name the
object and give its direction, for example "light, two points on the port
bow". Report everything, even if it was reported on the last watch. Stay on
your feet, stay at your post until relieved, and repeat a report until it is
acknowledged ([FM 55-501, para 2-30, pp. 2-9 to 2-10](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)).
Watch your own navigation lights too, and report at once if one is dim or out
(same source). For what other vessels' lights mean, see
[Navigation lights and day shapes](navigation-lights-and-shapes.html).

Keep distractions away from the steering position: no television or video in
view, phones put away. Make your drink before you take the watch, so that you
never leave the wheelhouse to make one
([EU guide, Module I, 9](../library/eu-guide-small-fishing-vessels.html);
[MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 26](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html)).
If the skipper works on deck while also on watch, they need a way to control
the boat and see around it from the deck
([EU guide, Module I, 9](../library/eu-guide-small-fishing-vessels.html)).

## Handing over the watch

The relief arrives early, before the hour. On Army vessels the rule is 15
minutes, which gives time to pass on information and, at night, for the
relief's eyes to adjust to the dark. The person going off watch passes on
what they are tracking and any orders. The relief takes over only when they
understand the situation, and says so clearly ("I relieve you"). A helmsman
also hands over the course being steered
([FM 55-501, para 2-28, p. 2-9](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)).

A good handover on a small boat covers: course and speed, position and the
next danger, every vessel or light in sight and what it is doing, weather
trend, the state of the engine and bilge, and when to call the skipper.

## Seeing at night

Night is the hardest time for a lookout. Eyes react slowly in the dark and
see a moving object more easily than a still one. You often see a faint light
better by looking a little to one side of it, or just above it, rather than
straight at it. Scan the horizon slowly and regularly, in sections; you may
have to look near an object several times before you notice it
([FM 55-501, paras 2-32 to 2-34, p. 2-12](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)).

To protect your night vision (same source, para 2-35):

- Come up about 10 minutes before your watch to let your eyes adjust.
- Use only a dim red light when you need light.
- Do not stare at instrument screens, even red-lit ones; turn them down.
- Use binoculars and keep all glass clean.
- Fatigue, alcohol and tobacco all reduce night vision.

## Fatigue: the main enemy

In more than a quarter of the collisions in the French study, drowsiness or
fatigue was noted for the person on watch on one or both vessels. In about
95% of cases the fishing boat's wheelhouse was manned by one person alone,
who could also be distracted by electronics or by work on deck
([BEAmer 2001, sections 14 and 15](../library/beamer-abordages-peche-commerce-2001.html)).
The study also found that:

| Finding | Source section |
| --- | --- |
| About three-quarters of collisions happened in good visibility, when people relax | 9 |
| Collisions on the way out and while fishing were equally common (42% each); passage watches were sometimes left to the least experienced crew | 11 |
| 45% happened at night; the middle and end of the night have the worst record | 12 |
| Mondays, the first day back at sea, had the most collisions | 13 |
| Last-minute avoiding action rarely worked | 24 |

The lesson of the last line: act early. A watch alarm (a "dead man" alarm
that sounds if not reset) helps, and should be switched on, working and loud
enough to be heard throughout the boat, but it is not a substitute for rest
([MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 92](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html);
[EU guide, Module IV, 5](../library/eu-guide-small-fishing-vessels.html)).
That EU case study describes a skipper who slept about two hours a day for
four days, fell asleep at the wheel on autopilot and put his trawler on a
well-marked rock half a mile from harbour.

Plan rest the way you plan fuel. Fatigue builds up over days, and the
consequences can be much worse than lost fishing time
([MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 40](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html)).
Two people on watch watch each other as well as the sea, and are unlikely to
fail at the same moment
([BEAmer 2001, section 15](../library/beamer-abordages-peche-commerce-2001.html)).

## Fishing boats and the "privileged" position

The French study found that many fishermen confuse being the stand-on vessel
with having priority. Nearly a quarter of the collisions involved a vessel
counting on its stand-on position instead of watching. In a traffic
separation scheme or narrow channel a fishing boat must not impede through
traffic. If you must use VHF to agree a manoeuvre and language is a problem,
the study suggests going through the coastguard
([BEAmer 2001, sections 19, 20, 22, 23](../library/beamer-abordages-peche-commerce-2001.html)).
See [Using a VHF radio](vhf-radio-procedure.html).

## Anchor watch

At anchor someone still keeps watch for a dragging anchor, changes in the
weather, other boats dragging or anchoring close, and the rode's fastening
([BCH, J.19, p. 6-130](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
Check the strain on the cable often, and check your position with bearings
of fixed objects; sound the fog signal if fog closes in
([FM 55-501, para 2-36, p. 2-13](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)).
If the compass heading stays the same but the bearings change, the anchor is
dragging ([BCH, J.17, p. 6-130](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
See [Anchoring](anchoring.html).
