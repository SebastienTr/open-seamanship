A passage plan is a step-by-step picture of the whole trip, **from berth to
berth**, made before you leave. It lets you see problems while you are calm
and ashore, not when you are tired and wet. Bowditch describes it as building
a "mental model" of the voyage, against which you measure progress the whole
way ([Bowditch, section 2807](../library/bowditch.html)). The US Coast Guard
notes that planning ahead cuts the workload when things happen fast
([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, p. 3-106](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).

## 1. Know your boat

Write down the numbers that decide where you can go
([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.2](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)):

- **draught** (the deepest point, with the boat loaded);
- **air draught** (height of the mast or highest point above the water);
- length and beam;
- **speed** at your usual engine revolutions, and **fuel consumption** per
  hour. Measure them: run a known distance both ways at fixed revolutions,
  and measure the fuel used in an hour
  ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.14-F.15, pp. 3-128 to 3-129](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).

## 2. Gather charts and information

- The **largest-scale charts** for the whole route, latest editions,
  corrected from the Notices to Mariners
  ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.5](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html);
  [Bowditch, section 2811](../library/bowditch.html)). See
  [Reading a nautical chart](reading-a-chart.html).
- **Sailing directions or pilot books** for the coast and harbours: they
  describe dangers, channels, anchorages and local rules that a chart cannot
  show ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.3](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
- **Light lists**, **tide tables and tidal stream data**, the **rules of the
  road**, and current **navigational warnings** (broadcast and local
  Notices to Mariners) (same section).
- Check that every piece of navigation equipment you need is on board and
  working, **including the non-electronic backups**: paper charts, compass,
  hand-bearing compass, dividers, plotter ([Bowditch, section 2810](../library/bowditch.html)).

## 3. Draw the route

1. Mark the dangers first: with your draught known, highlight the depth
   contour you must not cross, and mark rocks, wrecks, shoals and narrow
   places along the way ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.5.f, p. 3-114](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
2. Choose **waypoints** that keep you well clear. Check every leg for land,
   shallows and obstructions; a GPS route drawn point to point can cross
   land ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.6](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
   Enter waypoints at the dock and have someone else check them.
3. Draw the legs. Write the course (magnetic, or true and magnetic) and the
   distance on each ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.8](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html);
   [Bowditch, section 2811](../library/bowditch.html)). See
   [Compass, variation and deviation](compass-variation-and-deviation.html).
4. Mark **bridges and power lines** with their clearance, against your air
   draught ([Bowditch, section 1102](../library/bowditch.html)).
5. At each turn, note a **turn bearing**: a bearing of a charted object close
   to abeam that tells you when to turn
   ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.9](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
6. Near each danger, draw a **danger bearing** (NMT or NLT) or danger range,
   hatched on the dangerous side ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.10](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html);
   [Bowditch, section 1102](../library/bowditch.html)). See
   [Fixing your position without GPS](position-fixing.html).
7. Note **warning and danger soundings** for shallow stretches: a depth at
   which you slow down and check, and a depth at which you turn back
   ([Bowditch, section 1102](../library/bowditch.html)).
8. Mark where you will change charts, and where you must call a harbour or
   traffic service ([Bowditch, section 1102](../library/bowditch.html)).
9. Pick **bolt-holes**: harbours or anchorages along the route you can reach
   in bad weather, by day and by night. Plan to make for shelter if the
   weather turns ([FM 55-501, para 4-67](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)).

In a channel, plan your track on the starboard side to leave room for traffic
coming the other way ([Bowditch, section 1102](../library/bowditch.html)).
Avoid traffic separation schemes where you can, or plan to cross them at
right angles ([Rules of the road](rules-of-the-road.html)).

## 4. Time it: tides, streams and daylight

- Work out the **height of tide** at every shallow point and bar, at the time
  you will be there, from the official tables. Some places can be crossed
  only near high water ([Bowditch, section 1104](../library/bowditch.html)).
- Work out the **tidal streams**: use them in your favour, and plan to pass
  headlands, races and bar entrances near slack water or with wind and
  stream together ([Tides and tidal streams](tides-and-tidal-streams.html)).
- Calculate **time = distance ÷ speed** for each leg, using speed over the
  ground ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.11](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html);
  [Time, speed and distance](../tables/time-speed-distance.html)). Write
  the expected time at each waypoint. Decide whether you will arrive in
  daylight.

## 5. Weather

Get a forecast for the whole route and for the time the trip will take, plus
the outlook ([Bowditch, section 1105](../library/bowditch.html)). Decide
before you leave the wind strength and direction at which you will not go,
or will turn back. In poor visibility, plan for more lookouts, lower speed, or
waiting outside until it clears (same section). See
[Reading the weather at sea](reading-the-weather.html).

## 6. Fuel, water, crew

- Carry enough fuel to get there, cope with the unexpected, and get back or
  to a refuelling place ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.14, p. 3-128](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
- Leave the plan, the crew list and your expected time of arrival with
  someone ashore who will alert the rescue services if you are overdue (see
  "Uncertain" above and your regional rescue page).
- Plan watches so that nobody is exhausted; see
  [Keeping watch](night-watchkeeping.html).
- **Brief the crew**: the route, the dangers, the weather, who does what,
  safety equipment and what to do in an emergency
  ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.18, p. 3-130](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html);
  [Bowditch, section 2807](../library/bowditch.html)).

## 7. Under way: follow and check the plan

- Compare your actual position with the plan often, and correct course and
  speed to stay on track ([Bowditch, section 2809](../library/bowditch.html)).
- Cross-check electronics against each other and against what you see:
  marks, transits, depths ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.17](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
- Set alarms (depth, cross-track, anchor) to useful values, not so many that
  they become noise ([Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, F.16](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
- Keep the log: time, position, course, speed, weather, barometer.
- If things change (weather, a failure, a tired crew), **plan again** rather
  than pressing on ([Bowditch, section 2809](../library/bowditch.html)).
