## Situation

A gale is forecast, or the wind and sea are building and the boat is
starting to slam, roll heavily, or surf and sheer in following seas. What is
heavy weather depends on your boat and crew: a sea that is easy for one boat
is dangerous for another ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, p. 6-82](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

## Do now

**Prepare before it arrives** ([FM 55-501, para 4-67](../library/fm-55-501-marine-crewman.html)):

1. Fix your position and plot it. Note time, heading and speed.
2. Decide where to go: sheltered water if you can reach it safely **before**
   the worst. Otherwise, **sea room**: get away from a lee shore, headlands,
   shoals and harbour bars. Breaking seas on shoals and bars can capsize a
   boat even when the open sea looks moderate
   ([Bowditch, section 3805](../library/bowditch.html)).
3. Brief the crew: what will happen, who does what, stay low, move little.
4. Everyone in lifejackets and foul-weather gear. Rig jackstays; anyone on
   deck wears a harness clipped on.
5. Close all hatches, ports and windows. Lash or stow everything loose, above
   and below.
6. Pump the bilges dry, and keep doing it; sloshing water reduces stability.
7. Ready hand pumps, bailers, first aid kit, the liferaft and grab bag,
   sea anchor or drogue. Review abandon-ship drills.
8. Switch to a full fuel tank if you can. Show navigation lights. Keep
   listening to forecasts on VHF. If you may need help later, tell the
   coastguard your position and plans now, while the radio and crew still
   work (see [Mayday](../emergency/mayday-distress-call.html)).

**Handle the seas (powered boats)** ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, F.2-F.18, pp. 6-82 to 6-91](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)):

9. **Head seas:** slow down so the bow rises to each wave instead of
    burying. Take the seas about 30° to 45° off the bow. Never let the boat
    leave the water off a crest. Use just enough power to get over each crest,
    ease off down the back, add a little power in the trough to keep
    steering.
10. **Beam seas:** avoid them. Zigzag across the seas at an angle, each leg
    as long as possible. If a sea looks like it will steepen or break,
    turn bow-on to it at once.
11. **Following seas:** the stern can be thrown sideways (broach). Watch
    astern constantly. Ride on the **back** of a wave, adjusting speed so you
    do not overtake it or surf down its face.
12. To turn round in big seas, slow for a few seconds, then put the helm
    hard over and apply power, so you are beam-on for the shortest time. Do
    not start a sharp turn on the face of a steep wave.

**Storm tactics (sailing boats)** ([Wikipedia, "Heaving to", "Drogue", "Sea anchor"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)):

13. **Heave to**: back the jib to windward, ease the main, lash the helm so the
    boat tries to turn into the wind. She lies quietly, drifting slowly to
    leeward. Allow sea room for the drift.
14. Other tactics: run before the seas slowed by a **drogue** streamed
    astern, which keeps the stern to the seas and stops the boat surfing and
    broaching; or lie to a **sea anchor** from the bow, which holds the bow to
    the seas. Use a long, stretchy line (nylon) to absorb the shock loads.

## Why

Most damage and capsizes come from **breaking** waves striking a boat
beam-on, or from a broach in a following sea. A breaker as high as the boat's
beam can roll her; the steepness of a wave is as dangerous as its height
([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, p. 6-96](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
A current against the waves makes them shorter, higher and more likely to
break ([Bowditch, section 3804](../library/bowditch.html)), so a tide race or
a river bar is far worse than the open sea. A change of heading or speed can
stop heavy rolling or pitching that builds up when waves meet the boat in
step with her own motion ([Bowditch, section 3813](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Check that it worked

- The boat rides over the seas without slamming, burying the bow or
  shipping green water.
- Rolling is bearable; the boat does not lie beam-on.
- In following seas she stays straight and does not surf down wave faces.
- The bilge stays dry; everyone is clipped on, warm and accounted for.
- You know your position and have sea room downwind.

## If that fails

- **Knockdown or rollover:** hold on and hold your breath. A self-righting
  boat (such as a rescue lifeboat or a ballasted keelboat) should come back up
  within seconds: stay with her and do not try to swim out in that time; the
  propeller may still be turning. **If she stays upside down** (most motor
  boats, multihulls and dinghies do), unclip your harness and get out at once,
  then stay with the hull: see [Capsized or swamped](../emergency/capsized-or-swamped.html).
  When she comes up, head her to meet the next sea, count
  the crew, check for lines round the propeller, then check for damage, fire
  and water ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, G.2, p. 6-98](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Taking on water:** [Taking on water](../emergency/taking-on-water.html).
- **Engine failure near danger:** [Engine failure near rocks or a lee shore](../emergency/engine-failure-near-danger.html).
- **Life in danger:** call for help early, while the radio still works:
  [Mayday](../emergency/mayday-distress-call.html).

### Tropical storms

Avoid them: follow the official warnings and keep well clear of the forecast
track. If caught, find which side of the storm you are on. In the Northern
Hemisphere the right-hand side of the track, looking where the storm is
going, is the **dangerous semicircle**; in the Southern Hemisphere, the left.
If the wind shifts clockwise (Northern Hemisphere), you are probably in the
dangerous semicircle. Ships there put the wind on the **starboard bow** and
make as much way as possible; in the other semicircle, wind on the
**starboard quarter**. In the Southern Hemisphere, use the port side. If in
doubt, stop briefly to see how the wind shifts
([Bowditch, section 4015](../library/bowditch.html)).
