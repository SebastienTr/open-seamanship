The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, called the
COLREGs or the rules of the road, apply on the high seas and on all waters
connected to them that seagoing vessels can use. A government may add special
rules for its harbours, rivers and inland waters
([USCG Navigation Rules, Rule 1](../library/uscg-navrules.html)). This page
puts the main rules in plain words, with Rule numbers so you can check the
exact text in the [library copy](../library/uscg-navrules.html). It does not
replace the official text.

## First principles

**Good seamanship comes first.** Nothing in the rules excuses a skipper who
neglects a precaution that ordinary practice of seamen would take. You may
depart from the rules when you must, to avoid immediate danger
(Rule 2).

**Keep a proper lookout** at all times, by sight and hearing and by every
other means you have, such as radar and AIS, so that you can judge the
situation and the risk of collision (Rule 5). Most collisions start with
nobody watching: see [Keeping watch](night-watchkeeping.html).

**Go at a safe speed**: a speed at which you can take proper action and stop
in time. What is safe depends on visibility, traffic, how your boat handles,
background lights at night, wind, sea and current, and the depth of water
under you (Rule 6).

## Is there a risk of collision?

Use every means you have to decide. **If in doubt, assume there is a risk**
(Rule 7(a)).

The basic test is the compass bearing. Take a bearing of the other vessel
with a hand-bearing compass, or line it up with a fixed part of your own boat
while you hold a steady course. Take it again a minute or two later. **If the
bearing does not change much while the range closes, you are on a collision
course** (Rule 7(d)(i)). Risk can still exist when the bearing does change,
for example with a very large ship, a tow, or a vessel at close range
(Rule 7(d)(ii)). Do not decide on scanty information, especially scanty radar
information (Rule 7(c)). On taking bearings, see
[Quartermaster 3 & 2, ch. 10](../library/navedtra-quartermaster-3-2-1976.html).

## How to take avoiding action

Any action you take must be (Rule 8):

- **early**: made in ample time;
- **large**: a change of course or speed big enough for the other vessel to
  see at once, by eye or on radar. A string of small changes is wrong;
- **safe**: it must not lead you into a new close-quarters situation, and it
  must result in passing at a safe distance;
- **checked**: keep watching until the other vessel is past and clear.

With enough sea room, a large early alteration of course is often the best
action. If you need more time, slow down, stop or go astern (Rule 8(c) and
8(e)).

## Who gives way

The vessel that must keep out of the way is the **give-way vessel**. It must
take early and substantial action to keep well clear (Rule 16). The other is
the **stand-on vessel**. It keeps its course and speed (Rule 17(a)(i)).

| Situation | Who keeps clear | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You are overtaking (coming up from more than 22.5° abaft the other's beam; at night you would see only her stern light) | You, the overtaking vessel, whatever kind of vessel you are. If in doubt, assume you are overtaking | 13 |
| Two power-driven vessels meet head-on or nearly so | Both turn to starboard and pass port to port. If in doubt, assume it is head-on | 14 |
| Two power-driven vessels cross | The one that has the other on her own starboard side keeps clear, and avoids crossing ahead | 15 |
| Two sailing vessels, wind on different sides | The one with the wind on her port side keeps clear | 12(a)(i) |
| Two sailing vessels, wind on the same side | The windward one keeps clear of the leeward one | 12(a)(ii) |
| Sailing vessel with the wind to port, unsure what the windward boat is doing | Keeps clear | 12(a)(iii) |

For Rule 12, the windward side is the side opposite to the one on which the
mainsail is carried (Rule 12(b)).

**Overtaking stays overtaking.** If you are overtaking, a later change of
bearing does not turn you into a crossing vessel. You keep clear until you are
past and clear (Rule 13(d)).

### The order of priority between kinds of vessel

Except in narrow channels, traffic separation schemes and overtaking (Rules 9,
10 and 13 still apply), Rule 18 sets a pecking order. Each vessel in this
list keeps out of the way of those **above** it (a power-driven vessel keeps
clear of all the others):

1. **Not under command** and **restricted in her ability to manoeuvre**
   (cannot get out of the way).
2. **Engaged in fishing** (with nets, lines or trawls that restrict
   manoeuvrability; trolling lines do not count, Rule 3(d)).
3. **Sailing vessel** (under sail only; a sailing boat with her engine running
   is a power-driven vessel, Rule 3(b) and (c)).
4. **Power-driven vessel**.

A fishing vessel keeps clear of the first group only "so far as possible"
(Rule 18(a) to (c)). Any vessel other than one
not under command or restricted in her ability to manoeuvre should, if
circumstances allow, avoid impeding a **vessel constrained by her
draught**, and that vessel must navigate with particular caution
(Rule 18(d)).

### When the stand-on vessel must act

As stand-on vessel, you may act alone as soon as it is clear that the
give-way vessel is not doing what the rules require (Rule 17(a)(ii)). When
collision can no longer be avoided by the give-way vessel alone, you **must**
take the action that best helps avoid it (Rule 17(b)). In a crossing
situation, a power-driven stand-on vessel should not, if it can avoid it,
turn to port for a vessel on her own port side (Rule 17(c)). If you are unsure
what the other vessel intends, sound at least five short rapid blasts
([Sound and light signals](sound-signals.html), Rule 34(d)).

## Narrow channels and traffic separation schemes

In a narrow channel or fairway, keep as near to the outer limit on your
starboard side as is safe (Rule 9(a)). A vessel under 20 m (66 ft) or a sailing
vessel must not impede a vessel that can navigate safely only inside the
channel (Rule 9(b)). A fishing vessel must not impede any other vessel
navigating in the channel (Rule 9(c)). Do not cross a channel if that
impedes a vessel that can navigate safely only inside it (Rule 9(d)).

In a traffic separation scheme, use the correct lane in the direction of its
traffic flow and keep clear of the separation zone. Join or leave at the end
of a lane where you can. Avoid crossing lanes; if you must, cross on a heading
as near as practicable to a right angle to the traffic flow. Vessels under
20 m, sailing vessels and fishing vessels must not impede vessels following a
lane (Rule 10). Inshore traffic zones are for small craft, sailing vessels,
fishing vessels and local traffic (Rule 10(d)).

## In fog and poor visibility

Rule 19 applies when vessels cannot see each other in or near an area of
restricted visibility. The give-way table above does **not** apply then,
because it needs vessels in sight of one another.

- Go at a safe speed for the visibility; have the engine ready for immediate
  manoeuvre (Rule 19(b)).
- If you detect a vessel by radar alone, decide early whether a close-quarters
  situation is developing. If you alter course, avoid turning to port for a
  vessel forward of the beam (unless you are overtaking it), and avoid turning
  towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam (Rule 19(d)).
- If you hear a fog signal apparently forward of your beam, or cannot avoid a
  close-quarters situation with a vessel forward of the beam, slow to the
  least speed at which you can steer, and take all way off if necessary, until
  the danger is over (Rule 19(e)).

On a small boat: slow down, show lights, sound your fog signal
([Sound and light signals](sound-signals.html)), post a lookout away from
engine noise, listen for surf too, and consider anchoring out of the traffic
([Boat Crew Handbook, p. 5-9](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
