Weather sinks more small boats than anything else. The first defence is a
**forecast before you go and regular forecasts while at sea**; the second is
**your own eyes and a barometer**, because forecasts can be late and local
weather can change fast. The US Coast Guard puts it simply: listen to
official forecasts, add local knowledge, and check your own "hunches" against
a barometer or thermometer rather than trusting them alone
([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.22, p. 5-11](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

## Getting forecasts and warnings

- **Before you leave:** the national weather service forecast for your sea
  area, including any warnings, and the outlook for the next day or two.
- **At sea:** VHF weather broadcasts by the coastguard, **NAVTEX** on
  518 kHz (printed warnings and forecasts within about 200 miles of the
  coast), and offshore SafetyNET or HF radiofax
  ([Bowditch, section 3927](../library/bowditch.html)). Note the times of the
  broadcasts for your area and listen to every one.
- **Warnings are broadcast at once**, between scheduled bulletins, usually
  announced first on the distress and calling channel
  ([Hawaii weather guide, p. 3](../library/noaa-mariners-weather-guide-hawaii.html)).
- In US practice a **gale warning** means sustained winds of 34 to 47 knots
  (Beaufort 8 to 9) and a **storm warning** 48 knots or more
  ([Hawaii weather guide, p. 6](../library/noaa-mariners-weather-guide-hawaii.html);
  [Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, Table 5-2](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
  Gusts are stronger than the mean. See the [Beaufort scale](../tables/beaufort-scale.html).

Forecasts get less reliable the further ahead they look: a 12-hour forecast
is better than a 24-hour one ([Bowditch, section 3926](../library/bowditch.html)).
Far offshore, with few reports, a mariner who reads his own observations can
sometimes forecast the next few hours better than a forecaster ashore
([Bowditch, section 3928](../library/bowditch.html)). Regional pages list
national services, for example [France: marine weather bulletins](../regions/france-weather-bulletins.html)
and [UK: weather and safety broadcasts](../regions/uk-safety-information.html).

## The barometer

Read the barometer and **write it in the log every hour**, with the wind and
sky. The trend matters more than the number. Average sea-level pressure is
about 1013 hPa ([Bowditch, section 3910](../library/bowditch.html)).

- A **falling** barometer warns of an approaching low and probably stormy
  weather. A slow-moving, deep low can start the barometer falling days ahead
  ([Bowditch, sections 3912 and 3926](../library/bowditch.html)).
- A **sharp rise** after a fall often follows a cold front: clearer, colder
  air, often with strong, gusty wind ([Bowditch, section 3912](../library/bowditch.html);
  [Aerographer's Mate, p. 200](../library/navedtra-aerographers-mate-1976.html)).
- In the tropics, pressure clearly below normal for the season and place can
  warn of a tropical storm nearby ([Bowditch, section 4014](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Highs, lows and the wind

Wind blows from high to low pressure, turned by the earth's rotation. In the
Northern Hemisphere it circles **anticlockwise round a low** and
**clockwise round a high**; the reverse in the Southern Hemisphere. The closer
the isobars on a weather map, the stronger the wind
([Bowditch, sections 3910-3911](../library/bowditch.html)).

**Buys Ballot's law:** stand with your **back to the wind**. In the Northern
Hemisphere the low is on your **left**; in the Southern Hemisphere, on your
right ([Bowditch, section 3911](../library/bowditch.html)). Watching how the
wind changes tells you where the low is going.

Highs usually bring light winds and fair weather; lows bring cloud, rain and
strong winds ([Bowditch, section 3911](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Fronts: what to expect in a typical low

In the middle latitudes, lows usually travel eastwards with a **warm front**
ahead and a **cold front** behind ([Bowditch, section 3912](../library/bowditch.html)):

| Stage | Sky | Barometer | Wind (N. Hemisphere) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Warm front approaching | High thin cirrus, then a veil (cirrostratus), then thicker grey layers lowering, then steady rain | Falling | Usually freshening |
| Warm front passes | Rain stops or turns to drizzle, low cloud, poor visibility, maybe fog | Fall slows or steadies | Veers (turns clockwise) |
| Warm sector | Overcast or partly cloudy, moist, hazy | Steady | Steady |
| Cold front passes | Heavy cumulus and cumulonimbus, squalls, heavy showers, sometimes thunder | Rises, often quickly | Veers sharply, gusty |
| Behind the cold front | Clearing, cold, good visibility, showers | Rising | Often very gusty |

Squalls can come along or up to 200 miles ahead of a cold front
([Bowditch, section 3912](../library/bowditch.html)). A low passing on your
**poleward** side gives the sharp changes above; a low passing on the
equator side gives a gradual change ([Bowditch, section 3912](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Local winds: sea breeze and land breeze

On sunny days the land heats faster than the sea. From late morning, cool air
from the sea flows in: the **sea breeze**, strongest in mid-afternoon. A calm
morning can become a rough afternoon, and boats that went out in flat water
cannot get home. After sunset the land cools and the breeze dies; a light
**land breeze** may blow offshore at night
([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.2, p. 5-3](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html);
[Aerographer's Mate, pp. 306-307](../library/navedtra-aerographers-mate-1976.html)).

## Thunderstorms, squalls and waterspouts

- A towering, dark cloud with an anvil top (cumulonimbus) can bring sudden
  strong, shifting winds, heavy rain or hail, lightning and rough, confused
  seas ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.5, p. 5-6](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Distance:** count the seconds from the flash to the thunder; every
  5 seconds is about 1 mile (1.6 km)
  ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.7, p. 5-7](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Lightning:** head for shelter early; keep the crew low and inside; do not
  touch metal fittings or the radio; after a strike, expect the compass to be
  wrong and electronics damaged ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.8, p. 5-7](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Waterspouts** hang from cumulus or cumulonimbus clouds, mostly in warm
  waters but also at higher latitudes. Keep away
  ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.9, p. 5-8](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

## Fog

- **Advection (sea) fog** forms when warm, moist air blows over colder water.
  It can be thick, last for days and does not burn off in the sun. More wind
  can lift it into low cloud, or sometimes thicken it
  ([Bowditch, section 3917](../library/bowditch.html);
  [Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.11, p. 5-8](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Radiation fog** forms over land on clear, calm nights, drifts out of
  rivers and harbours in the early morning, and usually clears by midday. It
  is seldom found more than about 10 miles offshore
  ([Bowditch, section 3917](../library/bowditch.html)).
- **Sea smoke** rises when very cold air moves over warmer water
  ([Bowditch, section 3917](../library/bowditch.html)).

What to do in fog is on [Rules of the road](rules-of-the-road.html) and
[Sound and light signals](sound-signals.html).

## Cold: icing

In freezing air, spray freezes on deck, rigging and superstructure. The ice
keeps building as long as freezing spray continues; the extra weight high up
makes a boat less stable and can capsize her, and decks become slippery
([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, A.16, p. 5-10](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html));
see [Stability on a fishing boat](fishing-vessel-stability.html).

## Signs of a tropical storm

In tropical seas, in the storm season, watch for: an unusually **long swell**
from one direction (often the first sign); cirrus streaming from one point of
the horizon; a dark bank of cloud (the "bar") on the horizon; and a barometer
below normal and falling ([Bowditch, section 4013](../library/bowditch.html)).
Get the official tropical storm warnings and act early; see
[Heavy weather](heavy-weather.html).
