**Tide** is the vertical rise and fall of the sea. **Tidal stream** (called
tidal current in US books) is the horizontal flow that goes with it. The tide
rises and falls; the stream floods and ebbs. They are related, but they do
**not** turn at the same time in most places
([Bowditch, section 3601](../library/bowditch.html)).

> **Take tide times, heights and stream rates only from official tables**
> (the tide tables, tidal stream atlas or chart of the hydrographic office for
> your area, for this year). Do not guess them from the Moon, from another
> port, or from this page. Tide predictions take into account effects no rule
> of thumb can ([Bowditch, section 3608](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Why tides happen

The Moon, and to a lesser degree the Sun, pull on the oceans. The Sun's effect
is a little under half the Moon's ([Bowditch, section 3602](../library/bowditch.html)).
At most places there are two high and two low waters a day (a
**semidiurnal** tide). Some places have one of each (**diurnal**), and many
have a **mixed** tide with a big difference between the two highs or the two
lows of the same day ([Bowditch, section 3605](../library/bowditch.html)).
A semidiurnal tide repeats about every 12 hours 25 minutes, so high water
comes about 50 minutes later each day ([Bowditch, sections 3609-3610](../library/bowditch.html)).

### Springs and neaps

- **Spring tides** come around new and full Moon, when Sun and Moon pull in
  line: higher high waters, **lower** low waters, stronger streams.
- **Neap tides** come around the Moon's first and last quarters: smaller
  range, weaker streams.

The cycle takes about two weeks, and the effect can lag a day or two behind
the Moon. "Spring" has nothing to do with the season. There is more water at
neap low water than at spring low water ([Bowditch, sections 3608-3609](../library/bowditch.html)).
Tidal streams are strongest at springs and weakest at neaps
([Bowditch, section 3617](../library/bowditch.html)).

The tide does not rise at a steady rate. It starts slowly, is fastest around
half tide, and slows again near high water; the same on the ebb
([Bowditch, section 3604](../library/bowditch.html)). The
[Rule of twelfths](../tables/rule-of-twelfths.html) is a rough way to
picture this between two predicted heights.

## Chart datum and the depth of water

Depths on the chart are measured from **chart datum**, a low level chosen so
that the water seldom falls below it. Tide tables give heights above the same
datum ([Bowditch, sections 3612 and 3624](../library/bowditch.html)).

**Depth of water now = charted depth + height of tide now.**

Example: charted depth 4.5 m; tide table height at that time 1.2 m; depth
about 5.7 m. If the table shows a **negative** height (−0.3 m), there is
**less** water than charted: 4.2 m ([Bowditch, section 3624](../library/bowditch.html)).
Then subtract your draught and keep a safety margin for waves, squat and
error. A drying height (underlined on the chart) is subtracted instead: a
rock drying 1.5 m is covered by 0.5 m when the tide is 2.0 m.

Clearances under bridges and cables are given above a **high-water** level,
usually mean high water, so there is less room at a big spring high tide
([Bowditch, section 3613](../library/bowditch.html)).

### Weather changes the tide

Predictions assume normal weather. Water stands **higher** than predicted
with a low barometer or a strong onshore wind, and **lower** with a high
barometer or a strong offshore wind. For a stationary low, sea level rises
roughly 1 cm for every hectopascal below 1010 hPa
([Bowditch, sections 3624 and 3626](../library/bowditch.html);
[Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, B.24, p. 5-30](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
Where the tidal range is small, weather can matter more than the tide itself.

## Tidal streams

- Offshore the stream is often **rotary**: it turns steadily round the
  compass during the tide. In rivers, straits and channels it is
  **reversing**: flood one way, ebb the other, with **slack water** at the
  turn ([Bowditch, section 3615](../library/bowditch.html)).
- **Set** is the direction the stream flows **towards** (the opposite of how
  wind is named). **Drift** or **rate** is its speed in knots
  ([Bowditch, section 3615](../library/bowditch.html)).
- Slack water is **not** always at high or low water. In a long or narrow
  inlet, the stream may still run hard at high water outside; times can be
  hours apart ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, B.20, p. 5-29](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html);
  [Bowditch, section 3619](../library/bowditch.html)).
- Big range does not always mean strong streams, and the reverse
  ([Bowditch, section 3620](../library/bowditch.html)).
- The stream usually turns earlier near the shore than in mid-channel, runs
  fastest in mid-channel or on the outside of a bend, and may run differently
  near the bottom than at the surface ([Bowditch, sections 3621-3622 and 3627](../library/bowditch.html)).
- River flow, rain and wind add to or take away from the tidal stream; after
  heavy rain or drought the predictions can be well out
  ([Bowditch, sections 3618 and 3627](../library/bowditch.html);
  [Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, B.16](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

Predicted slack water is usually within half an hour of the real time. To use
a slack or a fair stream through a strait or entrance, plan to arrive at least
half an hour before the predicted time ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, B.24, p. 5-30](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

## Wind against tide

When wind and waves run against a strong stream, the waves get **shorter,
steeper and higher**, and can break. This is how tide races and overfalls
become dangerous, even in moderate wind. With the stream, waves get longer
and lower ([Bowditch, section 3804](../library/bowditch.html)). Plan to cross
races, headlands and bar entrances at slack water or with wind and stream
together. More on this in [Heavy weather](heavy-weather.html).

## Where to find the numbers

- **Tide tables**: times and heights of high and low water at reference
  ports, and corrections (time and height differences) for secondary ports
  ([Bowditch, sections 3628-3630](../library/bowditch.html)).
- **Tidal stream tables or atlases**, and the tidal stream notes or tables
  printed on many charts, give the set and rate hour by hour from high water
  at a reference port ([Bowditch, section 3619](../library/bowditch.html);
  [Boat Crew Handbook - Navigation, pp. 3-109 to 3-110](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-navigation.html)).
- Use the station nearest to you and apply its corrections exactly as the
  table explains ([Boat Crew Handbook - Seamanship, B.22](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

If you have no tables at all, Bowditch gives a crude method from the Moon's
meridian passage and the port's mean lunitidal interval
([Bowditch, section 3628](../library/bowditch.html)). Treat any result as a
rough guess and keep a large margin.
