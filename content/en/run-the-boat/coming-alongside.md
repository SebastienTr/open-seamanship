## Situation

You are about to berth against a quay, pontoon or another moored boat, or to
leave one, often with wind or current pushing you on or off. Or you need to
go alongside another vessel at sea to pass gear or people.

## Do now

**Before the approach**

1. Look at the wind and current at the berth itself; they may differ from
   what you had outside. Plan to approach against the wind and current and,
   if you can, berth on the side sheltered from the wind ([BCH, D.1, p. 6-68](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
2. Rig fenders and lead bow, stern and spring lines well before you arrive,
   with a spare line and a loose fender ready. Too many fenders is better
   than too few ([BCH, D.1 and E.4, pp. 6-68 and 6-77](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
3. Tell the crew which line goes where and who does what. Give orders loudly
   and clearly. Crew wear lifejackets when boarding or leaving
   ([BCH, D.1, p. 6-68](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html);
   [MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 20](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html)).

**Berthing**

4. Go slowly: just enough way to steer and to stem the wind and current. Use
   short bursts of power to turn without gaining speed
   ([BCH, D.1 and D.3.c, pp. 6-68 and 6-73](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
5. **Wind blowing you off the berth**: approach at a steep angle, 45° or
   more ([BCH, D.3.k, p. 6-74](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
6. **Wind blowing you onto the berth**: come in parallel, stop just off, and
   let the wind push you on. Have no fore-and-aft way on at contact; put
   fenders where the hull will touch (same page).
7. The springs stop her surging ahead or astern; bow and stern lines hold
   her in ([BCH, D.2.c, p. 6-71](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
8. Make fast the bow line, stern line and both springs. Add breast lines in
   bad weather (same page).

**Leaving with the wind pushing you on**

9. Use a spring to swing one end out
   ([BCH, D.2.d and D.3.h, pp. 6-72 and 6-74](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)):
   - **To swing the stern out**: keep only a spring from the bow led aft to the
     berth, put a fender at the bow, turn the helm towards the berth and go
     gently ahead.
   - **To swing the bow out**: keep only a spring from the stern led forward,
     fender the quarter, and go gently astern.
10. Rig the last line as a slip: both ends on board, the bight round the
    bollard, so you can let go one end and haul it in without help ashore
    ([BCH, D.2.e, p. 6-72](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
11. Haul lines in fast and keep them away from the propeller. Never leave a
    line unattended on a cleat while manoeuvring (same page).

**Alongside a vessel under way**

12. Agree the plan with the other skipper; ask a big ship to slow down rather
    than alter course ([BCH, E.1 and E.2.a, pp. 6-76 to 6-77](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
13. Approach from astern and, usually, on the lee side. Match speed, then
    close at 15° to 30° so you move sideways at no more than half your forward
    speed. Keep clear of the other vessel's propeller, rudder and quarter wave
    ([BCH, E.3 and E.5, pp. 6-77 to 6-78](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
14. Touch with your forward sections, about halfway between bow and amidships,
    and hold there with gentle helm and power. Stay as short a time as you
    can, or make fast ([BCH, E.7 and E.8, pp. 6-80 to 6-81](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
15. To leave, put the stern in slightly to bring the bow out, add a little
    power and steer away parallel. **Never go astern to clear a vessel that is
    making way** ([BCH, E.9, p. 6-81](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

## Why

Wind and current are the strongest forces at slow speed; use them rather than
fight them. A boat pivots about a point roughly one-third of her length from
the bow, and the stern swings the opposite way to the bow, so check the stern
will clear before you turn. Keep the stern away from danger: with the
propeller and rudder intact you can usually work out of trouble
([BCH, D.2.a, D.3.g, D.3.n and D.3.o, pp. 6-69 and 6-73 to 6-75](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
A spring held at one end turns engine power into a turning force, which is
why it can pull a boat off a berth that the wind is pressing on.

## Check that it worked

- At contact the boat is stopped, parallel and on her fenders, not surging.
- Springs stop her moving forward and back; bow and stern lines hold her in.
- When leaving, the end you swung out is well clear before you motor away.

## If that fails

- If the approach goes wrong, abort early: go astern or round and try again.
  If another vessel alters course while you are closing, break off and start
  again ([BCH, E.2.c, p. 6-77](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- **Never fend off with hands or feet**: use a fender
  ([BCH, D.3.j, p. 6-74](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
  A person between boat and quay can be crushed.
- To push off another boat or ship, a short burst astern and then neutral
  sends prop wash forward between the hulls and pushes you away
  ([BCH, D.3.i, p. 6-74](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).
- A line round the propeller: stop the engine. See
  [Rope or net round the propeller](../fix-it/fouled-propeller.html).
- If conditions are beyond you or your crew, wait or go elsewhere. The
  handbook's advice is to know your limits and not to push them
  ([BCH, D.3.s and D.3.t, p. 6-75](../library/uscg-boat-crew-handbook-seamanship.html)).

When boats lie alongside each other, keep your deck safe for others to cross:
not slippery, handholds in place, a clear route
([MCA Fishermen's Safety Guide, p. 21](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html)).
