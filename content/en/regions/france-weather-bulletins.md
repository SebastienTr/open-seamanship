French marine forecasts are written by **Météo-France** and broadcast at
sea by the **CROSS**, the coastguard and rescue centres
([Wikipedia FR, 'Centre régional opérationnel de surveillance et de sauvetage', 'Informer'](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
A VHF radio is the simplest way to receive them, and from 6 miles off
shelter the rules require one, plus a way of receiving marine forecasts
([Division 240, art. 240-2.05](../library/fr-legi-division-240.html)).

## How the CROSS broadcasts work

- **Announced on channel 16, read on another channel.** The CROSS first
  announce a broadcast on VHF channel 16 (or 2182 kHz on MF, or by DSC),
  then read it on a working channel. Switch to the channel they give and
  listen ([Wikipedia FR, 'Fréquences attribuées'](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
- **Coastal forecasts on VHF.** The bulletins for coastal waters are read
  on VHF several times a day, at set times or in a loop, from radio sites
  all along the coast. French Wikipedia names channels 79 and 80 as the
  usual ones, depending on the area (see the Uncertain box).
- **Offshore forecasts on MF.** Centres that cover a GMDSS sea area A2 also
  read offshore (*large*) bulletins on MF.
- **Special bulletins (BMS).** A *bulletin météorologique spécial* warns
  that wind of **force 7 or more near the coast, or force 8 or more
  offshore**, is blowing or expected. The CROSS broadcast it when needed,
  again announced on channel 16
  ([Wikipedia FR, 'Bulletin météorologique spécial'](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
- **Navigation warnings (AVURNAV).** Urgent warnings about dangers to
  navigation are broadcast too, including by NAVTEX.

Keeping a [watch on channel 16](../run-the-boat/vhf-radio-procedure.html),
as the rules ask ([art. 240-2.07](../library/fr-legi-division-240.html)),
means you hear these announcements as well as calls for help.

## NAVTEX

NAVTEX is a small receiver that prints or displays text warnings and
forecasts. The international service is on **518 kHz in English**; a
country may add a service in its own language on **490 kHz**. Range is
about 300 miles. In France, NAVTEX broadcasts are coordinated by the CROSS.
Each message starts with a station letter and a subject letter (A for
navigational warnings, B for weather warnings, and so on), so you can
choose what to receive ([Wikipedia FR, 'Navtex'](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
See also [GMDSS and DSC](../run-the-boat/gmdss-and-dsc.html).

## Reading a French bulletin

French bulletins name sea areas. Some names cover more than their
geography suggests: for example the weather area "Iroise" runs well
beyond the Iroise sea, down to the Vilaine estuary
([Wikipedia FR, 'Mer d'Iroise'](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). Know the
names of the areas you will cross. Wind is given on the Beaufort scale:
see [Beaufort scale](../tables/beaufort-scale.html), [Sea state](../tables/sea-state.html)
and [Reading the weather at sea](../run-the-boat/reading-the-weather.html).

## What to prepare before you sail

1. **Write down the broadcast times** for your area and the channels or
   radio sites used. Get them from Météo-France's current *Guide Marine*
   or from the CROSS. Times change, so take a fresh copy each season.
2. **Know the area names** in the bulletins you will hear.
3. **Have the day's tide times and coefficients** (required everywhere
   except the Mediterranean, [art. 240-2.03](../library/fr-legi-division-240.html)).
   See [Tides and tidal streams](../run-the-boat/tides-and-tidal-streams.html).
4. **Check your radio**: VHF on, squelch set, volume up, listening on
   channel 16.
5. **Plan a way out** if a special bulletin comes: which shelter you can
   reach in time ([Planning a passage](../run-the-boat/passage-planning.html)).

## If you hear a special bulletin at sea

- Note the time, area, wind force and direction, and the timing.
- Decide early: reef, change course or head for shelter. See
  [Heavy weather](../run-the-boat/heavy-weather.html).
- If the situation becomes dangerous, call the CROSS on channel 16
  ([Service-Public.fr, F33954](../library/fr-service-public-fiches.html)):
  see [France: CROSS, SNSM and calling for help](france-search-and-rescue.html).

In Brittany, strong tidal streams make wind against tide especially
dangerous: see [Brittany: the coast, tides and races](brittany-coast.html).
