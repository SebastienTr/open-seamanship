## Situation

A sail has split across the cloth, a seam has opened, a batten pocket or a
patch has torn, or chafe has worn a hole. A small tear in a loaded sail grows
fast. This page covers stopping it, then mending it well enough to finish the
passage.

## Do now

1. **Take the load off the sail at once.** Ease the sheet or bear away so the
   torn part is not pulling, then reef, roll away or drop it.
2. **Do not let it flog.** A sail that shakes hard in a strong wind can split
   from end to end and can damage the spar
   ([Knight, pp. 375-376](../library/knight-modern-seamanship-1903.html)).
   If you cannot drop it quickly, keep it full and drawing gently until you
   can.
3. **Set a smaller or different sail** so you can keep steering and moving:
   a reef in the mainsail, the storm jib, or the engine. See
   [Heavy weather](../run-the-boat/heavy-weather.html).
4. **Get the sail down on deck or below.** Spread it out flat, torn part
   uppermost
   ([Verrill, ch. VI](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)). Rinse salt off
   the damaged area with fresh water if you have it, and dry it.
5. **Stop the tear running.** Put sail-repair tape over the whole tear on
   both sides, with 5 cm (2 in) to spare at each end. Round the corners of
   the tape so they do not peel. On a short passage in light weather, tape
   may be all you need.
6. **Sew it for strength.** Use a sail needle, a sailmaker's palm to push it,
   and waxed polyester twine. Work from the ends of the tear inwards so it
   cannot run
   ([Admiralty Manual, pp. 117-120](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)).
   - **Open seam:** follow the old stitch holes where you can, with a round
     seam (one edge folded over the other). The Admiralty spacing of about
     112 stitches to the yard is about one stitch every 8 mm (1/3 in).
   - **Straight tear in the cloth:** pull the edges together without
     overlapping and sew across them in a zig-zag (herringbone) stitch, about
     every 12 mm (1/2 in).
   - **Hole or long tear:** sew a patch over it, about twice the size of
     the damage, with rounded corners and its edges turned under. Sew round
     the edge of the patch, then trim and sew the edge of the hole to the
     patch from the other side.
   - **Small hole:** darn it, without a patch
     ([Admiralty Manual, p. 120](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)).
7. **Check what caused it** before you set the sail again: a spreader end,
   a split pin, a sharp shackle, or the sail rubbing on a shroud. Tape or pad
   the cause.

## Why

Sailcloth fails where load meets weakness: a worn seam, a chafed patch or
cloth weakened by years of sunlight. Once the weave breaks, the load that the
broken threads carried passes to the next threads, which then break too.
Taking the load off is the only way to stop it. Stitches spread the load
again across many threads, which is why spacing matters. Modern sails are
usually woven polyester, or laminates that tear differently
([Wikipedia, 'Sail'](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Check that it worked

- Hoist the repaired sail reefed or partly rolled, in moderate wind, and
  watch the repair for a few minutes.
- The repair lies flat, with no puckering pulling on the cloth around it, and
  no stitch has pulled through.
- Check the repair again after each watch.

## If that fails

- Keep the sail down and sail or motor on what you have. A boat can reach
  port under mainsail alone, headsail alone, or engine. See
  [Steering failure](steering-failure.html) for balancing a boat with one
  sail.
- If the mast or rigging was damaged too, see
  [Broken shroud, stay or mast](rig-failure.html).
- Have the sail properly repaired by a sailmaker when you reach port. See
  [Sails and rigging](../know-your-boat/sails-and-rigging.html).
- For knots and lashings to secure a damaged sail, see
  [Ropes and knots](../know-your-boat/ropes-and-knots.html).
