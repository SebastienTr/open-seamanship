## Situation

You pump and the bowl will not empty, the handle is very stiff or goes down
with no resistance, or what you pumped out comes back into the bowl. Marine
toilets use small pumps and valves that are easily blocked by too much
paper or anything fibrous
([Wikipedia, 'Head (watercraft)'](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Do now

1. **Stop pumping hard.** Forcing a blocked pump can split a hose or burst a
   joint below the waterline, which is a worse problem than a blocked toilet.
2. **Check the seacocks.** Both the water inlet and the waste outlet seacocks
   must be open to use the toilet. A closed outlet seacock is a common cause
   of a "blockage".
3. **Holding tank fitted?** Check the valve that sends waste to the tank or
   overboard is in the right position. A full tank, or a blocked tank vent,
   stops the pump. If the tank vent is blocked, pressure can build up in the
   tank: clear the vent before pumping more.
4. **Put on rubber gloves**, eye protection, and old clothes. Have a bucket,
   rags and plenty of water to rinse with.
5. **Close both seacocks** before opening anything. Pump the bowl as empty as
   you can, and bail out the rest.
6. **Open the pump and the outlet valve.** Most hand-pumped toilets have a
   one-way valve (a rubber flap or "duckbill") where the waste leaves the
   pump. A check valve like this only lets flow one way
   ([Fluid Power, ch. 4](../library/navedtra-fluid-power-1970.html)). If
   paper or debris holds it open, waste flows back into the bowl; if it is
   stuck shut, the pump will not empty. Clean it or fit a spare.
7. **Check the discharge hose.** Feel along it: a hard, narrow section is a
   blockage or scale. Take it off at the easier end and clear it with a
   flexible rod or by tapping and flexing the hose.
8. **Reassemble**, open the seacocks, and test with clean water before use.

## Why

A marine toilet is a small pump with one-way valves, pushing waste through a
long hose that often rises above the waterline before going to a tank or out
through the hull. Anything that holds a valve half open, or narrows the hose,
stops it working. Most blockages are paper, wipes or sanitary products, so
the best fix is prevention: only what has been eaten, and little paper, goes
in the bowl. Put a notice up for guests.

Some boats can discharge into the sea and some must keep waste in a holding
tank until it can be pumped out ashore. Under IMO rules for ships, untreated
sewage should not be discharged within 12 nautical miles (22 km) of land
([Wikipedia, 'Marine sanitation device'](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Local rules for small craft vary.

## Keep it from sinking the boat

A toilet bowl is often close to or below the waterline. With the seacocks
open, a damaged valve or a siphon in the hose can let the sea run back in,
slowly, until the boat floods. Close both seacocks when you leave the boat,
and when the toilet is not in use at sea if the sea is rough. Make sure every
seacock can be reached and closed quickly, even with water over it
([MCA guide, PDF p. 30](../library/mca-fishermens-safety-guide.html)). See
[Through-hulls and seacocks](../know-your-boat/through-hulls-and-seacocks.html).

## Check that it worked

- Ten strokes of clean water go out with normal effort, and the bowl stays
  empty afterwards.
- No water rises in the bowl when you stop pumping.
- No drips at the pump, valves or hose joints. Check again after an hour.

## If that fails

- **Cannot clear it at sea:** close both seacocks, label the toilet "out of
  use", and use a bucket. Clean the bucket with sea water over the lee side.
- **Water rising in the bowl with the seacocks closed:** the leak is not
  through the toilet. Look elsewhere and see
  [Taking on water](../emergency/taking-on-water.html).
- **Hose split below the waterline:** close the seacock. If it will not
  close, see [Taking on water](../emergency/taking-on-water.html).
- **Bilge smells of sewage:** a hose or tank is leaking. Ventilate the boat,
  wash with plenty of water, and wash hands carefully before handling food.
