## Floating

A body floats if it weighs less than the water it would push aside if fully
under water. It sinks until the water it pushes aside (its **displacement**)
weighs exactly as much as it does
([Reed, *Stability of Ships*, p. 1](../library/reed-stability-of-ships-1885.html)).
Load a boat and she sinks deeper until she has pushed aside enough extra water.
Let water in and the same thing happens, until there is no more hull above the
water to give buoyancy. Then she sinks. That is why **freeboard** (the height of
the deck edge above the water) is reserve buoyancy
([MCA, *Fishing Vessel Stability Guidance*, p. 13](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

## Two centres

- **G, the centre of gravity**: the single point where the whole weight of
  the boat and everything in her acts downward. It moves only if weight moves,
  is added or is removed. Rolling does not move it.
- **B, the centre of buoyancy**: the centre of the underwater volume, where the
  water's upward push acts. It **moves** as the boat heels, towards the low
  side, because that side now has more hull in the water.

([MCA, pp. 7-9](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html))

When the boat heels, G stays where it is and B moves out to the low side. The
weight pushes down through G and the buoyancy pushes up through B. Because they
are no longer in line, they make a turning force that rolls the boat back
upright. The horizontal distance between the two lines is the **righting lever,
GZ** ([Reed, pp. 2-3](../library/reed-stability-of-ships-1885.html);
[MCA, glossary](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

If G is so high that B cannot move out past it, the forces turn the boat
further over instead: she capsizes
([Reed, pp. 2-3](../library/reed-stability-of-ships-1885.html)).

## What gives stability

([MCA, p. 14](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html))

1. **A low centre of gravity.** Weight low down, especially ballast in a deep
   keel.
2. **Beam.** A wide boat's B can move far out sideways, so she is stiff at
   small angles.
3. **Freeboard.** A high side keeps hull in the water at large angles, so B
   keeps moving out and the boat keeps righting.

Sailing boats use these two ways. A narrow, deep boat with her ballast low
heels easily at first, but the further she heels the more her ballast levers
her back up; many are "practically uncapsizable". A beamy, shallow boat resists
heeling at first, but after a certain angle she will capsize
([Knight, *Sailing*, ch. III](../library/knight-sailing.html)). Dinghies,
multihulls and motor boats rely on beam; keelboats mostly on ballast.

The **GZ curve** shows the righting lever at each angle of heel. Raising G
shrinks the whole curve: less righting at every angle, and capsize at a
smaller angle ([MCA, pp. 25-28](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

## Stiff and tender

A **stiff** boat rolls back quickly; a **tender** one rolls slowly, and is at
greater risk. A slow, lazy roll is a sign that the centre of gravity is too high
([MCA, pp. 10 and 29](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html);
[Wikipedia, "Metacentric height", "GM and rolling period"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
For fishing vessels, the MCA gives a rule of thumb: roll period in seconds
(one full roll, side to side and back) less than the beam in metres means
stiff; more than the beam means tender
([MCA, p. 29](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

Too stiff is uncomfortable: a boat with too much lead on her keel snaps back
violently and strains her gear ([Knight, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)).

## What makes a boat less stable

([MCA, pp. 10 and 16-21](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html))

- **Weight high up.** A small weight high up has the same effect as a large
  one low down: a dinghy on the coachroof, a radar arch, people on the
  flybridge, a heavy catch on deck.
- **Suspended loads.** A load hanging from a crane, boom or davit acts at the
  point it hangs from, not where the load is. When the boat heels, it swings
  out and adds to the heel.
- **Weight off-centre.** A list does not correct itself until the weight is
  moved, and a listing boat has less righting left on that side.
- **Free surface.** Liquid that can slosh (in a part-full tank, the bilge, or
  on deck) runs to the low side as she heels and takes its weight with it.
  This always reduces stability, whatever the depth of the liquid. Long, wide
  slack tanks are worst; full or empty tanks are best
  ([MCA, pp. 17-18 and 32-33](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html);
  [Wikipedia, "Metacentric height", "Free surface effect"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
  Water in the boat is doubly dangerous: it is free surface and it uses up
  freeboard. Pump it out; keep freeing ports clear.
- **Open hatches and doors.** They let water in at large angles. Keep them
  shut at sea.

**List and loll.** A list comes from weight on one side. A loll comes from
weight too high: the boat will not stay upright and flops to one side or the
other. To correct a loll: lower weights, add weight low down starting on the
low side, remove weight high up, and get rid of free surfaces
([MCA, p. 20](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

## Small and multihull boats

EU law asks that a habitable multihull that could capsize stays afloat upside
down, and that small boats under 6 m that can be swamped have enough flotation
to stay afloat swamped
([Directive 2013/53/EU, Annex I, 3.3](../library/eu-rcd-2013-53.html)). Stay
with a capsized boat that floats: see
[Capsized or swamped small boat](../emergency/capsized-or-swamped.html).
