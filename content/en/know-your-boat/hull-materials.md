## Glass-reinforced plastic (GRP, fibreglass, FRP)

Most boats built since the 1960s are GRP: glass fibre cloth or mat set in
resin, laid into a mould. Boats have been made this way since the early 1940s
([Wikipedia, "Fiberglass", "Boating"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). The
outer surface is a coloured resin layer, the **gelcoat**. Inside, frames,
stringers, floors and bulkheads stiffen the shell
([FAO 2025, pp. 13-15](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).
Some hulls and many decks are **cored**: two thin GRP skins on either side of
foam or balsa ([Wikipedia, "Hull (watercraft)", "General features"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

GRP does not rot or rust, but it does fail. What an inspector looks for
([FAO 2025, pp. 12-13](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)):

- **Soft patches** or panels that flex when pressed: broken laminate or
  delamination (layers separating).
- **Blisters** below the waterline ("osmosis").
- **Whitening or dry glass** showing through: too little resin.
- **Cracks**, especially at hard spots, round fittings and where the boat has
  hit quays or beaches. Fix cracks promptly or the damage spreads.
- **A soft transom**, which carries the outboard or engine loads.
- **Fittings without backing plates**: cleats and stanchions need a plate
  behind them to spread the load.
- **Waterlogged buoyancy foam** in small boats.

**Osmosis.** Gelcoat lets a little water through but not what is dissolved in
it. Water collects behind it and builds up pressure, raising a blister
([Wikipedia, "Osmotic blistering"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). A few small
blisters are common; a hull covered in them needs a surveyor's opinion.

Care: wash, wax the gelcoat, keep the bilges dry, repair damage early, and use
gelcoat to finish small surface repairs
([FAO 2025, p. 12](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).

## Wood

Wooden hulls are planks on frames, held by fastenings, with seams caulked
watertight ([Wikipedia, "Hull (watercraft)"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Modern wooden boats may be plywood or strip-planked and sheathed in GRP.

- **Rot** is the enemy. Its most frequent cause is lack of ventilation
  ([Knight, *Sailing*, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)). Keep the boat
  dry and aired; let air reach every part of the structure. Probe suspect wood
  with a spike: soft wood is rotten.
- **Fastenings** corrode, and the planks then work loose ("nail sickness").
  The minimum standard is galvanised steel
  ([FAO 2025, p. 16](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).
  Heavy weights resting on the planking rather than the frames also loosen it
  ([Knight, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)).
- **Caulking and seams** must stay watertight. A wooden boat out of the water
  dries and opens her seams; she may leak for some days after launching.
- Protect wood with oil or paint and keep it clean
  ([FAO 2025, p. 16](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).

A GRP skin over wood hides rot underneath. Inspectors may strip suspect areas
to look at the wood ([FAO 2025, p. 21](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).

## Steel

Steel is strong, tough in a collision and easy to repair by welding almost
anywhere. Its enemy is **rust**, especially at welds, the keel and the bilge
([FAO 2025, p. 21](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).
Steel hulls need anodes on the hull and stern gear
([FAO 2025, p. 22](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html);
[Corrosion and anodes](corrosion-and-anodes.html)), and a sound paint system
inside and out. Hidden places (under tanks, behind linings, in the bilge) rust
first.

The repair sequence for a rust patch
([FAO 2025, p. 22](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)):
chip off the rust; inspect the plate and seams round it; wire-brush to bare
metal; sand; prime with metal primer; topcoat. Plate thinned by rust needs a
welder.

## Aluminium

Aluminium is light and, kept clean and dry, resists corrosion well
([FAO 2025, p. 24](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).
But it is the most active of the common boat metals. Joined to stainless steel,
bronze or copper in sea water, the aluminium is eaten
([Wikipedia, "Galvanic corrosion", "Examples"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
So on an aluminium boat:

- watch every fitting of another metal, and keep them insulated;
- never use copper-based antifouling on the hull;
- check anodes and renew at about 70 per cent wasted;
- inspect welds and joints for **fatigue cracks**, and look into crevices
  where salt water can sit
  ([FAO 2025, p. 24](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html));
- take extra care with shore power and stray current.

## Mixing materials

Anything fixed through the hull must suit the hull. The UK code asks that a
distance piece between a seacock and the hull be compatible with both
([MCA code, 2.18.2](../library/mca-small-fishing-vessel-code-2024.html)).
See [Through-hulls and seacocks](through-hulls-and-seacocks.html).

When the hull is holed, go to [Taking on water](../emergency/taking-on-water.html).
