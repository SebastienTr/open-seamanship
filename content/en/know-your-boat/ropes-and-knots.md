## How rope is made

Fibres are twisted into **yarns**, yarns into **strands**, and strands into
rope, each stage twisted the opposite way so the rope holds together. Most
three-strand rope is **right-handed** (laid "with the sun")
([Verrill, *Knots, Splices and Rope Work*, ch. I](../library/verrill-knots.html)).
Laid rope partly untwists under load. Modern **braided** rope has a woven
jacket over a core
([Wikipedia, "Rope", "Construction"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Materials

| Fibre | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nylon (polyamide) | Anchor rodes, mooring lines, towlines: strong and stretchy, absorbs shocks | 10 to 15 per cent weaker wet; stores energy, so whips back hard if it breaks |
| Polyester | Sheets, halyards, general use: strong, low stretch, not affected by water | — |
| Polypropylene | Floating lines, rescue lines, cheap mooring pick-ups: light, floats | Weak and degraded by sunlight |
| High-modulus (UHMWPE, aramid) | Halyards, rigging: very strong, almost no stretch | Slippery; knots can slip |
| Natural (manila, hemp, coir) | Traditional work | Rots if stowed wet ([Admiralty Manual, p. 101](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)) |

([Wikipedia, "Rope"](../library/wikipedia-en.html))

## Looking after rope

- **Rinse and dry** before stowing; salt crystals cut fibres and damp rots
  natural rope.
- **Keep out of the sun** when not in use; ultraviolet light weakens many
  synthetics ([Wikipedia, "Rope"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
- **Prevent chafe.** Lead lines fair; use chafe guards where a line passes
  over an edge or through a fairlead.
- **Whip or seal the ends** so they do not unravel
  ([Verrill, ch. II](../library/verrill-knots.html)).
- **Coil laid rope clockwise** (right-handed) to avoid kinks
  ([Admiralty Manual, p. 131](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)).
  Coil braided rope in figure-eights or with alternate twists
  ([Wikipedia, "Rope", "Construction"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
- **Avoid shock loads**, and **stand clear of a line under heavy load**: if it
  parts, especially if it is stretchy, it can kill
  ([Wikipedia, "Rope"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
- **Retire a rope** that is stiff, glazed, badly chafed, or furry and weak to
  the touch.

## Words

The **standing part** is the main length; the **end** is what you tie with; a
**bight** is a loop or bend in the rope
([Verrill, ch. II](../library/verrill-knots.html)). A **bend** joins two ropes;
a **hitch** fixes a rope to a post, ring or other rope.

## The essential knots

| Knot | Use | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Figure-of-eight** | Stopper at the end of a sheet or halyard so it cannot run out | Easy to undo after loading |
| **Bowline** | A fixed loop: mooring loop, round a person, to a sail | Does not slip or jam; undoes easily. Not under load |
| **Round turn and two half hitches** | Mooring line to a ring, post or rail | The round turn takes the load while you tie the hitches; can be untied under load ([Admiralty, p. 62](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)) |
| **Clove hitch** | Fender to a rail; quick temporary hitch | Can slip on a smooth rail unless finished with a half hitch ([Verrill, ch. III](../library/verrill-knots.html)) |
| **Rolling hitch** | Take the strain off another rope, e.g. a jammed sheet on a winch | Grips when pulled along the rope ([Admiralty, pp. 60-61](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)) |
| **Sheet bend** (double for security) | Join two ropes, especially of different size | Double sheet bend holds better and jams less ([Admiralty, pp. 66-67](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)) |
| **Reef knot** | Tie reef points round a sail; bandages | Not for joining two ropes of different size: it slips and pulls apart ([Verrill, ch. II](../library/verrill-knots.html)) |
| **Fisherman's (anchor) bend** | Rope to an anchor ring | Two turns, then a half hitch through them, end stopped ([Admiralty, p. 64](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)) |

**Bowline, in brief** ([Admiralty, p. 67](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)):
make a small overhand loop in the standing part; bring the end up through it,
round behind the standing part, and back down through the small loop; hold the
end against the side of the big loop and pull the standing part tight. Leave a
good tail. The Navy used it "for slinging a man over the ship's side"; a
bowline on the bight makes a two-loop seat.

**Reef knot, in brief**: left over right and under, then right over left and
under. If the ends come out on different sides you have tied a "granny",
which slips or jams ([Verrill, ch. II](../library/verrill-knots.html)).

**Round turn and two half hitches**: pass the end twice round the ring or
post, then make two half hitches round the standing part, both turned the same
way ([Verrill, ch. III](../library/verrill-knots.html);
[Admiralty, p. 62](../library/admiralty-manual-seamanship-1915.html)).

## Which knot when it matters

- **Someone in the water**: a bowline in the end of a line, big enough to go
  under the arms. See [Man overboard](../emergency/man-overboard.html).
- **Towing**: bowlines or round turns that you can cast off under load. See
  [Towing and being towed](../run-the-boat/towing.html).
- **Anchoring on rope**: fisherman's bend, or a splice with a thimble. See
  [Anchoring](../run-the-boat/anchoring.html).
- **A line jammed on a winch**: rolling hitch from another line to take the
  load off. See [Sails and rigging](sails-and-rigging.html).
