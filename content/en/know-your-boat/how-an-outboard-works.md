## Two halves

An outboard is a complete engine with its gearbox and propeller, clamped to
the transom. It has two main parts
([TM 5-278, pp. 2-4](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)):

- **The powerhead** on top: cylinders, crankshaft, pistons, carburettor or
  injection, ignition and, on small motors, the fuel tank.
- **The lower unit** (the leg): a vertical drive shaft down to a pair of bevel
  gears, the propeller shaft, the water pump and the exhaust outlet. The gears
  set the reduction from engine to propeller
  ([TM 5-278, p. 13](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).

Unlike a diesel, an outboard runs on petrol (gasoline) and needs a **spark** to
light the fuel. So a petrol outboard needs fuel, air, compression, a spark at
the right time, and cooling water.

## Two-stroke and four-stroke

**Two-stroke** outboards do the whole cycle in one turn of the crankshaft. The
rising piston compresses the charge above it and draws fresh mixture into the
crankcase below it. After the spark, the falling piston uncovers the exhaust
port, then the transfer port, and the mixture squeezed in the crankcase rushes
into the cylinder ([TM 5-278, p. 4](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).
Every downward stroke is a power stroke, so two-strokes are light for their
power.

Because the mixture passes through the crankcase, the oil cannot sit in a sump.
It is **mixed with the petrol**. Too much oil fouls the plugs and sticks the
rings; too little scores the pistons and bearings
([TM 5-278, p. 5](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)). Some modern
two-strokes have an oil pump instead of pre-mixed fuel
([Wikipedia, "Two-stroke engine", "Two-stroke reversibility"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Two-strokes send
some unburnt fuel and oil out with the exhaust, which is why emission rules
have pushed them out of many markets
([Wikipedia, "Two-stroke engine", "Emissions"](../library/wikipedia-en.html);
["Outboard motor", "History and developments"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

**Four-stroke** outboards work like a car engine, with valves and an oil sump.
Most new outboards are four-stroke. The smaller ones are often carburetted;
the rest are electronically fuel-injected
([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Four-stroke outboards"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
**Never put two-stroke mix in a four-stroke, or plain petrol in a two-stroke
that needs mix.**

## Fuel

On a carburettor motor, fuel flows from the tank through the line to a float
chamber. Air rushing through the carburettor's narrow throat draws fuel through
small jets and breaks it into a mist
([TM 5-278, p. 7](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)). Those jets
are tiny; old fuel that has gone gummy, or water, blocks them. The tank has an
**air vent** in its cap. If it is closed, the motor starves
([TM 5-278, Trouble chart, p. 72](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).
Check the vent, the line connector and the primer bulb first on any portable
tank.

## Spark

Older and small motors make their own spark with a **magneto**: magnets in the
flywheel pass a coil, and when contact points open, a high voltage jumps the
spark-plug gap ([TM 5-278, pp. 8-9](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).
Modern motors use electronic ignition, but the spark plug is still the weak
point. Long slow running, too much oil or the wrong plug foul it with a black
deposit ([TM 5-278, p. 9](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).

The **kill cord** (lanyard) stops the motor if the helmsman goes overboard
([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Safety"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). A
missing or loose kill-cord clip is a common reason for a good outboard not to
start. Wear it whenever the motor runs.

## Cooling

Most outboards are cooled by raw water. A **rubber-impeller pump** in the leg
draws water through intake slots below the waterline and pushes it up through
the powerhead ([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Cooling system"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Water and exhaust leave through the leg or propeller hub. A small stream from
a hole in the cowling or leg, the **tell-tale**, shows that water is flowing.

The impeller is ruined if it runs dry for long, for example if the motor is
started out of the water or tilted up while running
([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Cooling system"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
The intakes must stay under water: a motor mounted too high, or a short shaft
on a tall transom, can overheat
([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Shaft length"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Some older motors used the pressure of water from the propeller instead of a
pump, and needed a burst of speed after starting to fill the jackets
([TM 5-278, pp. 9-10](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).

No tell-tale stream: see
[Outboard: no cooling-water stream](../fix-it/outboard-no-cooling-water.html).

## Propeller, trim and ventilation

A propeller is sized by **diameter** and **pitch**: how far it would move
forward in one turn if the water were solid. Real propellers **slip** 20 to
40 per cent. Heavy boats need larger, lower-pitch propellers; light fast boats
higher pitch ([TM 5-278, pp. 11-12](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)).

**Trim** is the angle of the motor to the hull. Trimmed out too far, the bow
rides high; too far in, the bow digs in and the boat steers badly
([TM 5-278, pp. 21-22](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html);
[Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Trim"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

If the motor suddenly races and the boat slows, the propeller is drawing air:
**ventilation**. Causes are a motor set too high or trimmed out too far, a
damaged anti-ventilation plate or propeller, or weed or rope on the propeller
([Wikipedia, "Outboard motor", "Ventilation"](../library/wikipedia-en.html);
[TM 5-278, pp. 12-13](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)). Many
propellers also have a rubber hub or a shear pin that slips or breaks when the
propeller hits something, to save the gears. See
[Rope or net round the propeller](../fix-it/fouled-propeller.html).

## What the old trouble chart still teaches

The 1944 chart sorts "motor fails to start" into ignition, fuel and mechanical
causes, and lists among fuel causes: wrong mixture, blocked line or screens,
water in the fuel, a closed tank vent and a flooded carburettor
([TM 5-278, pp. 71-72](../library/tm-5-278-outboard-motors-1944.html)). That
order (spark, fuel, then mechanics) is still the quickest way to find a fault.
Step by step: [Outboard won't start](../fix-it/outboard-wont-start.html).
