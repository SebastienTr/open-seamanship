## Why they matter

A boat floats because water is kept outside. Every hole through the hull
below the waterline, and many just above it, is a way in. Through-hulls feed
the engine, the toilet, the galley sink, the deck drains, the log and the
depth sounder. A single failed fitting or hose can sink a boat at her
mooring. When water is coming in, see
[Taking on water](../emergency/taking-on-water.html).

## The parts

- **Through-hull fitting**: the tube through the hull, with a flange outside
  and a nut inside.
- **Seacock**: the valve on top of it, fixed to the hull so it cannot be
  wrenched off. Lever in line with the pipe usually means open.
- **Hose tail** and **hose**, held by **clips**.
- Often a **strainer** (engine intake) or a **non-return valve** (discharges).

A seacock is "a valve on the hull of a boat or a ship, permitting water to flow
into the vessel ... or out of the boat"
([Wikipedia, "Seacock"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## What good looks like

The UK code for small fishing vessels gives a clear picture
([MCA code, 2.17 and 2.18](../library/mca-small-fishing-vessel-code-2024.html)):

- Every inlet and discharge has an **efficient means of closing it**, marked to
  show whether it is open or shut and what system it serves.
- The valve is **at the hull**, and **readily accessible in an emergency**. If
  it cannot be reached, it has an extended spindle or a pull wire from above
  the floor.
- Discharges (engine cooling, bilge pumps, galley and toilet drains) have a
  **non-return valve** next to the closing valve.
- Below the waterline, valves and pipes are **steel, bronze or another
  equivalent material compatible with the hull**.
- **Flexible hose** is used only where vibration needs it, joined with
  purpose-made clips, doubled where necessary, and all piping is supported
  against vibration and chafe.

An inspector checks bottom valves, sea-water piping and hoses on every survey
([FAO 2025, p. 42 and Annex A](../library/fao-small-fishing-vessel-inspection-2025.html)).

## Open or closed?

Seacocks on closed systems such as the engine's cooling water are normally
left open while in use. Seacocks to open things such as a sink drain may be
open in harbour but **closed at sea**: when the boat heels or rolls, the outlet
can go below the waterline and water flows in
([Wikipedia, "Seacock"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). The fishing guidance
says the same for every closure: keep it shut at sea when not in use, even in
good weather ([MCA, *Fishing Vessel Stability Guidance*, p. 19](../library/mca-fishing-stability.html)).

A simple rule: **if you are not using it and you are leaving the boat, shut
it.** And before you open any part of a sea-water system, shut its seacock
first ([Engineman 3, p. 501](../library/navpers-engineman-1957.html)).

## How they fail

| Failure | Cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seized valve | Never worked; marine growth; corrosion | Work every seacock open and shut regularly |
| Hose splits or comes off | Old, hard rubber; rusted clip; one clip only | Squeeze hoses; replace hard or cracked ones; use two clips where possible |
| Fitting cracks or weeps | Wrong metal; zinc leached from brass; galvanic attack | Replace with bronze or an approved material; see [Corrosion and anodes](corrosion-and-anodes.html) |
| Fitting breaks off | Knocked, or used as a step or handhold | Protect it; it should be strong enough to stand a knock |
| Water back-flows through toilet or engine | Outlet below the waterline when heeled; no loop or non-return valve | Close seacocks when unused; see [Blocked marine toilet](../fix-it/blocked-marine-toilet.html) |

## Keep a record

Make a sketch of the hull showing every through-hull, what it is for and how to
reach its seacock. Tape it inside a locker door. In the dark, with water
rising, nobody should have to search.

Many sailors keep a tapered wooden bung tied next to each through-hull, to
hammer into the hole if a fitting fails.
