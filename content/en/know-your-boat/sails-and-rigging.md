## Two kinds of rigging

- **Standing rigging** stays put and holds the mast up: the **forestay** in
  front, the **backstay** behind, and **shrouds** at the sides, often upper and
  lower, spread out by **spreaders**. Today it is mostly stainless steel wire,
  with swaged or mechanical end fittings
  ([Wikipedia, "Rigging", "Standing"](../library/wikipedia-en.html);
  [Verrill, *The Book of the Sailboat*, pp. 33-37](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)).
- **Running rigging** moves: **halyards** hoist sails; **sheets** trim them;
  **outhauls**, **downhauls**, **kickers (vangs)** and **topping lifts** shape
  and support them. It is now synthetic rope
  ([Wikipedia, "Rigging", "Running"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). See
  [Ropes and knots](ropes-and-knots.html).

Every stay pulls against another. The forestay and backstay balance each other
fore and aft; the shrouds on each side balance across. **If one fails, the mast
loses support on that side.** That is why the first move after a broken shroud
is to turn the boat so the broken side is to leeward, taking the load off it.
See [Broken shroud, stay or mast](../fix-it/rig-failure.html).

## Parts of a sail

([Knight, *Sailing*, ch. I](../library/knight-sailing.html))

| Part | Where |
| --- | --- |
| Head | Top corner (on a triangular sail) |
| Tack | Lower front corner |
| Clew | Lower back corner, where the sheet pulls |
| Luff | Front edge, along the mast or forestay |
| Leech | Back edge |
| Foot | Bottom edge |

Most modern sails are polyester (Dacron)
([Wikipedia, "Polyester", "Uses and applications"](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## How sails drive and turn the boat

A sailing boat can sail to within about 45° of the wind. The wind pushes the
boat forward, sideways (leeway) and over (heel). The keel or centreboard resists
the sideways push; ballast or beam resists the heel
([Knight, ch. III](../library/knight-sailing.html)).

The sails also turn the boat. If the after sail (mainsail) is more powerful,
the stern is pushed away and the bow turns into the wind: **weather helm**. If
the head sail is more powerful, the bow is pushed off: **lee helm**. A boat
should carry a little weather helm, so that if the helm is let go in a squall
she turns into the wind and stops. Too much weather helm makes steering hard and
drags the rudder ([Knight, ch. III](../library/knight-sailing.html)).

This explains several things you feel at the helm:

- Heavy weather helm in a gust means too much mainsail: reef the main or ease
  the mainsheet.
- With only a headsail up, the boat may be hard to keep close to the wind.
- If the helm suddenly goes light or heavy without a change of wind, look for
  a problem: a sail blown out, rope or weed on the rudder, or steering damage.
  See [Steering failure](../fix-it/steering-failure.html).

## Reefing

Reefing makes a sail smaller. On a slab-reefed mainsail you lower the sail
part way, fix the new tack (luff cringle) at the boom, haul the new clew (leech
cringle) down and out with its reef line, then tie in or tidy the loose sail
([Verrill, ch. V, p. 84](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)). Take one reef at a
time, and tie reef points with reef knots, round the sail, not round the boom
if you can avoid it. Many headsails today reef by rolling round the forestay.

The best rule is the oldest: **"Don't wait too long before reefing."** If the
boat heels badly, labours, carries hard helm, or the wind is gusty and strong,
reef at once. It is easier to shake a reef out than to put one in when it is
blowing hard ([Verrill, ch. V](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)). See
[Heavy weather](../run-the-boat/heavy-weather.html).

## Looking after the rig

**Standing rigging**

- Look at every end fitting (top and bottom), every split pin and every
  rigging screw. Look for broken wire strands, cracks in swaged terminals,
  rust weeping from fittings, and bent toggles.
- Stainless steel corrodes inside crevices where salt water sits still, such
  as inside a terminal or under a fitting, so it can look bright and still
  fail ([Wikipedia, "Crevice corrosion", "Mechanism"](../library/wikipedia-en.html);
  [Corrosion and anodes](corrosion-and-anodes.html)).
- Check the tension: the lee shrouds should not hang slack in normal sailing.
  Knight warned against setting wire up too tight on a wooden boat, as it
  strains the hull ([Knight, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)); modern
  rigs follow the maker's figures.
- Overhaul it every year: renew anything frayed, clean and protect metal
  ([Verrill, ch. V](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)).

**Running rigging**: look for chafe where lines pass sheaves, fairleads and
jammers; end-for-end or replace worn lines
([Verrill, ch. V](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)).

**Sails**

- Never stow a sail wet if you can help it. Mildew comes from damp and lack of
  air, and a sail cover does not keep damp out for ever
  ([Knight, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)).
- Sunlight weakens sailcloth and stitching; cover furled sails.
- Mend small tears and chafe at once; check stitching at corners, batten
  pockets and where the sail rubs the spreaders
  ([Verrill, ch. V](../library/verrill-sailboat.html)). See
  [Torn sail](../fix-it/torn-sail.html).
- New sails stretch: Knight's advice was to let them stretch evenly and not
  haul them out hard in a blow at first ([Knight, ch. IX](../library/knight-sailing.html)).
