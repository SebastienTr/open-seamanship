Navigation has always asked the same three questions: where am I, which way
is my destination, and how far is it? The answers changed with the tools.

## Pilots, charts and the first compasses

Written sailing directions existed by the 6th century BC. Charts may be as old
as 600 BC ([Bowditch, section 103](../library/bowditch.html)). The oldest
surviving sea charts are the Mediterranean portolan charts of the late 1200s
and early 1300s, drawn on vellum with networks of lines in 32 directions
([Wikipedia: Portolan chart](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). French
*routiers* appeared about 1500, the English called them "rutters", and in
1584 Lucas Waghenaer published *The Mariner's Mirror*, the model for pilot
books for generations ([Bowditch, section 103](../library/bowditch.html)).

The magnetic compass came from China, where pilots used a south-pointing
needle in dark weather by 1111-1117. The first European record, by Alexander
Neckam, dates from about 1190. In the Mediterranean the compass, with better
dead reckoning and charts, let ships keep the sea in winter; by about 1290 the
sailing season could run from February to December
([Wikipedia: History of the compass](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Compass errors took centuries to understand. Edmond Halley mapped variation in
the South Atlantic in the 1700s. Matthew Flinders found that a vertical iron
bar corrects some deviation, and after 1840 George Airy and later Lord Kelvin
worked out iron and magnet correctors. Kelvin's dry-card compass came in the
1870s, and the liquid-filled compass became standard in 1906. Iron and steel
ships made the gyrocompass necessary: Léon Foucault built the gyroscope in
1852, and the American Elmer Sperry and the German Anschütz-Kaempfe built electrical gyrocompasses
early in the 20th century ([Bowditch, section 103](../library/bowditch.html)).
For today's practice see [Compass, variation and deviation](../run-the-boat/compass-variation-and-deviation.html).

## Speed, distance and the lead line

Sailors first timed a chip of wood passing along the hull. Then came a board
on a knotted line, run out while a sand-glass emptied, which is why speed is
still given in knots. Mechanical logs arrived in the mid-1600s and the taffrail
log in 1878 ([Bowditch, section 103](../library/bowditch.html)). In 1901
Captain Lecky still advised marking 23 ft 4 in (7.1 m) on deck, the length of
one knot for a 14-second glass, to check the log line. He also printed Thomas
Gray's "Mariner's Creed": never neglect the **Lead, Log, Latitude and
Look-out** ([Lecky, pp. 186-187](../library/lecky-wrinkles-1901.html)). It is
still good advice.

## Measuring the sky

Portuguese and Spanish navigators of the late 1400s measured the Sun's noon
height with the mariner's astrolabe, a heavy graduated ring; the first known
description of how to make and use one was printed in 1551
([Wikipedia: Mariner's astrolabe](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Isaac Newton
designed a two-mirror reflecting instrument, shown to the Royal Society in
1699 but published only in 1742. John Hadley and Thomas Godfrey built the
same principle around 1731, giving the octant and then the sextant
([Wikipedia: Octant; Sextant](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Longitude needed
an accurate clock or the Moon; that story has its own page,
[Finding longitude](finding-longitude.html).

On 17 December 1837 the American captain Thomas Sumner was running for the
Irish coast in a gale with no sight for days. He worked one Sun sight with
three different latitudes and saw that the positions lay on a straight line
pointing at Small's light. He held his course and sighted the light within an
hour. This was the first celestial **line of position**
([Bowditch, section 110](../library/bowditch.html)). Later in the 19th century
the intercept method, credited to Marcq Saint-Hilaire, made plotting a line
quicker ([Wikipedia: Celestial navigation](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Not
everyone was convinced: in 1901 Lecky called this "New Navigation" a
fine-weather method, "not suited to oilskin weather"
([Lecky, pp. 391-392](../library/lecky-wrinkles-1901.html)). Precomputed
sight reduction tables began with Pub. No. 214 in 1936
([Bowditch, section 111](../library/bowditch.html)).

## Radio, radar and satellites

| Date | Step |
| --- | --- |
| 1904 | Radio time signals for checking chronometers |
| 1907 | Radio navigational warnings begin (US Hydrographic Office) |
| 1921 | First radiobeacon; radio direction finders in use |
| 1922 | First practical echo sounder (US Navy) |
| 1937 | First sea-going radar trials (USS *Leary*) |
| 1943 | US Coast Guard operating Loran chains |
| 1944 | Decca used by the Royal Navy for the D-Day minefields |
| 1945 | Radar available for merchant ships |
| 1978 | First GPS satellite launched |
| 1995 | GPS declared fully operational |
| 2000 | GPS "selective availability" switched off (2 May); UK Decca ends (31 March) |
| 2010 | Loran-C switched off in North America |
| 2015 | Loran-C switched off in France and Norway (31 December) |

Sources: [Bowditch, sections 112-115 and 2500](../library/bowditch.html);
[Wikipedia: Decca Navigator System; Global Positioning System](../library/wikipedia-en.html).

Decca was a favourite of fishermen in coastal waters after the war; Loran-C
covered the oceans ([Wikipedia: Decca Navigator System](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Both are gone. GPS grew from a US Department of Defense project started in
1973; after the shooting down of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 in 1983 it was
promised for civil use ([Wikipedia: Global Positioning System](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## The lesson

Bowditch notes that a generation ago the navigator worked alone with compass,
chart, sextant and chronometer, and now depends on systems run by others
([Bowditch, section 116](../library/bowditch.html)). Keep the old skills
alive: a [paper chart](../run-the-boat/reading-a-chart.html), a written log
and the ability to [fix your position without GPS](../run-the-boat/position-fixing.html).
When the screen goes dark, the lead, the log, the latitude and the look-out
still work.
