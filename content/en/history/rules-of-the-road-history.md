Two ships meeting at sea need to make the same choice at the same moment. For
centuries this rested on custom. Steam, speed and night traffic made custom
dangerous, and the rules we use today grew out of that problem, often one
collision at a time.

## Custom, then the first written rules

Before common rules, practices differed from place to place, lights were not
standard, and many sailing vessels showed no lights at all. In 1840 Trinity
House in London drew up rules that Parliament enacted in 1846, and lights for
steamers became law in 1848. In 1858 Britain recommended coloured sidelights
for sailing vessels and required fog signals: the whistle for steamers, a fog
horn or bell for sail
([Wikipedia: COLREGs, History](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

In 1863 new rules drawn up by the British Board of Trade, in consultation with
the French government, came into force. By 1864 more than 30 maritime nations
had adopted them, including the United States
([Wikipedia: COLREGs, History](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Stephen Luce's
*Seamanship* of 1866 shows what that looked like on deck:

- Steamers under way carried a white masthead light over 20 points, visible
  5 miles, and red and green sidelights over 10 points each, visible 2 miles,
  with inboard screens to stop them showing across the bow.
- In fog, steamers sounded the whistle, sailing ships a fog horn, and vessels
  at anchor a bell, at least every 5 minutes.
- A vessel at anchor showed one white all-round light visible 1 mile.
- The ship on the starboard tack kept her wind and the one on the port tack
  bore away, "without any regard being paid to rank of the respective
  commanders". Steamers gave way to sailing vessels.
- Luce adds that Britain, France, the Netherlands and Russia had ordered their
  ships to follow rules "of similar tenor".

([Luce, ch. XIX, pp. 363-365](../library/luce-seamanship-1866.html))

Luce also describes the hardest case, two lights converging at an angle, as one
"for which no general or certain rule can be laid down"
([Luce, p. 368](../library/luce-seamanship-1866.html)).

In 1867 Thomas Gray of the Board of Trade wrote *The Rule of the Road*, a
pamphlet famous for its memory verses
([Wikipedia: COLREGs, History](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). In 1901 Captain
Lecky wrote that "sailors of all nations owe a lasting debt" to Gray
([Lecky, p. 186](../library/lecky-wrinkles-1901.html)).

## Washington, 1889

In 1889 the United States hosted the first international maritime conference
on collision rules. Its rules were adopted in 1890 and came into force in 1897
([Wikipedia: COLREGs, History](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Austin Knight's
*Modern Seamanship* (1903) prints them with notes. Several rules a modern
sailor knows took shape there:

- Two steamers meeting end-on both alter course to starboard and pass port
  to port.
- In a crossing, the steamer that has the other on her starboard side keeps
  out of the way.
- The stand-on vessel now had to keep both course **and speed**.
- A new article: the give-way vessel should, if possible, avoid crossing
  ahead.

Knight called the last one "one of the most important in the Rules of the
Road". He also notes that "moderate speed" in fog filled more than a hundred
pages of the conference debates, and that courts had found steamers at fault
in dense fog even when running at under 5 knots
([Knight, ch. XIV, pp. 233-237](../library/knight-modern-seamanship-1903.html)).

## Radar, then the 1972 rules

Small changes followed in 1910. In 1935 nations agreed to give steering orders
by the rudder, not the tiller. A 1948 conference recognised radar, in rules
that took effect in 1954; new rules from a 1960 conference took effect in 1965
([Wikipedia: COLREGs, History](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Radar brought a new kind of accident. On 25 July 1956, in thick fog off
Nantucket, the liners *Andrea Doria* and *Stockholm* each tracked the other
only by radar, misjudged the other's course and collided; 51 people died.
Investigators found that proper radar plotting had not been done, and lines
were required to improve radar training
([Wikipedia: SS Andrea Doria](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). The lesson is
in today's rules: a radar contact must be plotted, not guessed.

The current International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea were
adopted on 20 October 1972 under what is now the International Maritime
Organization ([Wikipedia: COLREGs](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). The US Coast
Guard's 1977 edition notes that they came into force at 12 noon local time on
15 July 1977, replacing the 1960 rules, and that inland waters kept separate
national rules ([USCG CG-169, Foreword](../library/uscg-cg169-navrules-1977.html)).
They have been amended many times since; Annex IV on distress signals was
rewritten in 2007 ([Wikipedia: COLREGs](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Traffic lanes: Dover and Ushant

The 1972 rules gave legal force to traffic separation schemes. The first was
set up in the Strait of Dover in June 1967 and became compulsory in 1971.
Ushant, off western Brittany, was on the list published in November 1973.
Many ships ignored it at first, cutting across or using the inshore passage
([Wikipedia (fr): Rail d'Ouessant](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

On 16 March 1978 the tanker *Amoco Cadiz* lost her steering in a Channel gale
and drove ashore on the Portsall rocks, spilling about 220,000 tonnes of oil
([Wikipedia: Amoco Cadiz oil spill](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). France then
moved the Ushant lanes further offshore, gave tankers and dangerous cargoes
their own lane, gave the Navy powers to enforce it, and from 1979 watched it by
radar from Ouessant, under the CROSS at Corsen. A salvage tug was stationed
for ships in trouble ([Wikipedia (fr): Rail d'Ouessant](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

## Why it matters

Every rule on the [Rules of the road](../run-the-boat/rules-of-the-road.html),
[Navigation lights and shapes](../run-the-boat/navigation-lights-and-shapes.html)
and [Sound and light signals](../run-the-boat/sound-signals.html) pages has a
story like these behind it. Old books are worth reading for the reasoning, but
always follow the current rules.
