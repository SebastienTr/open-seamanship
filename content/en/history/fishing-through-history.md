Fishing is the oldest trade at sea and still one of the most dangerous. How
people fished, and how far they went, followed the boats and engines of each
age. This page follows that thread, with Brittany as one example among many.

## Salted fish and long voyages

Cod caught and dried in northern Norway, around the Lofoten islands, was sold
to southern Europe from the Viking period, more than 1,000 years ago. By the
end of the 1300s the Hanseatic League ran the trade from Bergen. The
Portuguese have fished cod in the North Atlantic since the 1400s
([Wikipedia: History of fishing, Cod trade](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Salt let fishermen go far from home. From the 1500s Basque, Portuguese,
English and French crews crossed the Atlantic every year to the Newfoundland
banks. Some dried their catch ashore on the coast; others salted it aboard on
the banks for six or seven months. From about 1873 the French fished from
dories, two men to a boat, working lines all day
([Wikipedia (fr): Terre-neuvas](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). For the
Breton side of this story see [Breton seafarers](breton-seafarers.html).

## Herring busses and sailing trawlers

In the 1400s the Dutch developed the herring buss, a sturdy decked vessel of
about 20 m with a crew of 18 to 30, fishing with long drift nets; it was used
until the early 1800s. In the 1600s the English dogger worked lines and early
trawls in the North Sea. In the early 1800s the fishermen of Brixham, in
Devon, developed a fast gaff-rigged sailing trawler that could tow a net far
offshore, and the trade spread to Hull, Grimsby, Scarborough and Yarmouth
([Wikipedia: History of fishing](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Inshore, small open boats did most of the work. Breton sardine boats around
1900 were open boats of about 10 m with two masts, sailing to the grounds and
rowing while they fished; Douarnenez alone had more than 800 of them
([Wikipedia (fr): Sardinier](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

Steam boats were longer, carried more nets, and fished with less regard for
wind and tide ([Wikipedia: History of fishing](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
They were also a threat to men with sailing boats. A steam trawler with 12 men
could catch as much as eight sailing dundees with 50 to 60 men, and the crew of
*Kerino* were driven away when they tried to land their fish at Concarneau
([Wikipedia (fr): Concarneau](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

## Oil engines in small boats

Engines came to smaller boats as oil engines. A 1914 British handbook says
that, apart from the big steam trawlers of Hull and Grimsby, the backbone of
Britain's sea-going fishing fleet was the Scottish Zulu and Fifie herring
boats, then being fitted with motors in numbers. The first Fifie with a motor,
*Pioneer* of St Monance, was said to have sailed 22 miles in two hours under
sail alone ([Marine Oil-Engine Handbook, ch. XVIII](../library/marine-oil-engine-handbook-1914.html)).
Most of these motors were hot-bulb or paraffin engines; in 1914 the true diesel
had made little headway in fishing vessels
([Marine Oil-Engine Handbook, chs. XI and XVI](../library/marine-oil-engine-handbook-1914.html)).
The diesel won later; see [The marine diesel engine](the-marine-diesel.html).

## The industrial fishery

The Second World War brought radar, echo sounders and radio aids within
reach of ordinary fishermen. In 1947 a surplus minesweeper was refitted in
Scotland as the first combined freezer and stern trawler, and in 1953 the
*Fairtry* was built at Aberdeen as the first purpose-built stern trawler,
hauling up to 60 tonnes over the stern. In the 1960s nylon nets, cheaper and
almost invisible in water, caught much more fish
([Wikipedia: History of fishing, Further development](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
Coastal fishermen took to radio navigation; Decca was widely used by fishing
vessels until it closed in 2000
([Wikipedia: Decca Navigator System](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

The result was a catch the stocks could not sustain. The northwest Atlantic
cod catch peaked at 810,000 tonnes in 1968, about three times the best year
before the factory trawlers. In 1992, with northern cod at about 1% of its
former level, Canada closed the fishery
([Wikipedia: Collapse of the Atlantic northwest cod fishery](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
French fishing at Newfoundland had already ended: the last Fécamp ships in
1987, the last Saint-Malo ship in 1992
([Wikipedia (fr): Terre-neuvas](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

## Rules and zones

Europe's first common fishing rules date from 1970. In 1976 the European
Community extended its fishing limits from 12 to 200 nautical miles, and after
exclusive economic zones were defined in 1982 the Common Fisheries Policy
proper was created in 1983, covering stocks, vessels, markets and agreements
with other countries ([Wikipedia: Common Fisheries Policy](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
