Brittany sticks out into the Atlantic between the Bay of Biscay and the
Channel. For centuries its people lived from the sea: as explorers and
privateers, naval sailors, deep-sea fishermen, and the men who built lights
on its rocks and went out in its lifeboats. This page gives some of the facts
behind that reputation. For the coast itself, see
[Brittany: the coast, tides and races](../regions/brittany-coast.html).

## Saint-Malo: Cartier and the privateers

Jacques Cartier (1491-1557), born in Brittany, sailed from Saint-Malo in 1534,
two years after the Duchy of Brittany was formally united with the French
crown. He was the first European to describe and map the Gulf of Saint
Lawrence, and gave the name "Canada" to the land he explored
([Wikipedia: Jacques Cartier](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Saint-Malo was also a great privateering port. Privateers (*corsaires*) were
private ships commissioned in wartime to take enemy merchant ships; the
prizes were sold and the proceeds shared. René Duguay-Trouin (1673-1736),
son of a Saint-Malo shipowning family, commanded a privateer at 18, joined the
Royal Navy as an officer, became a lieutenant general of the fleet, and
commanded the port of Brest from 1731
([Wikipedia (fr): René Duguay-Trouin](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

Robert Surcouf (1773-1827), also of Saint-Malo, raided British shipping in the
Indian Ocean during the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars and took more than
40 prizes, among them the East Indiaman *Kent* in 1800. The record is not only
heroic: he also traded in enslaved Africans, illegally at times, and later
financed slave ships. After 1815 he sent fishing expeditions to Newfoundland.
He is buried in Saint-Malo
([Wikipedia: Robert Surcouf](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Privateering
ended for the major European powers with the Declaration of Paris in 1856
([Wikipedia: Privateer](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Brest and the navy

Brest has been France's main Atlantic naval port for centuries. Its naval
college began afloat: from 1827 on the old ship of the line *Orion* in the
roadstead, named École navale by 1830, then on a series of ships called
*Borda* from 1840 to 1913. It moved ashore in 1914 and now stands at Lanvéoc,
on the south side of the roadstead
([Wikipedia (fr): École navale](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). Captain
Joseph de Bonnefoux commanded the *Orion* school ship from November 1835 to
October 1839. His editor, himself born in Brest, noted that most of the
ordinary seamen named in the memoirs were Bretons
([Bonnefoux, preface](../library/bonnefoux-memoires.html)).

## Cod: Newfoundland and Iceland

From the 1500s to the 1900s, French fishermen, many of them Bretons and
Normans, sailed each spring to the Newfoundland banks. Saint-Malo was a major
port. In the 19th century more than 10,000 French fishermen went every year.
Crews left in late February or March for six or seven months. From about 1873
they fished from dories, flat-bottomed boats with two men each, setting lines
all day; fog that cut the dories off from the ship was the main killer. In the
worst years 200 to 400 men died. A hospital-ship service began in 1896. The
last sailing *terre-neuvier* stopped in 1951, and the last Saint-Malo ship
fitted out for Newfoundland sailed in 1992
([Wikipedia (fr): Terre-neuvas](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

Bretons who fished off Iceland were called *Islandais*. From Paimpol and
nearby ports, 2,644 ships and 65,000 men sailed to Iceland between 1852 and
1935; about 120 schooners were lost and at least 2,000 men from the Paimpol
area drowned. The trade began to decline after 1907
([Wikipedia (fr): Paimpol](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

## Sardines and tuna

Along the south coast, sardines made towns. The first sardine cannery in
Douarnenez opened at Tréboul in 1853. By 1900 the town had 26 canneries,
employing nearly 3,000 people, mostly women, and 5,600 fishermen; its yards
built 176 boats that year ([Wikipedia (fr): Douarnenez](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
Concarneau followed the same path. In 1899 the first Breton steam trawler,
*Kerino*, entered service at Lorient; when her crew tried to land fish at
Concarneau they were driven off by local fishermen
([Wikipedia (fr): Concarneau](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

Tuna took Bretons further out. Groix was France's leading tuna port from 1870
to 1940 ([Wikipedia (fr): Thonier](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). Its
fishermen adopted the *dundee*, a ketch-rigged boat that by local account was
first rigged in 1883 by Pierre Baron after his open boat capsized and lost its
mast in the Raz de Sein; hulls grew from about 10 to 30 to 60 tonnes. A storm
in 1930 sank many dundees, and a new stern shape followed that also suited the
first engines ([Wikipedia (fr): Dundee (bateau)](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
In the Gulf of Morbihan, the two-masted *sinagots* of Séné fished and carried
cargo; they were open boats 10 to 12 m (33 to 39 ft) long, often crewed by two
people, sometimes women, and took engines from 1947
([Wikipedia (fr): Sinagot (bateau)](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).

Life ashore was hard too. From 1900, Jacques de Thézac opened *Abris du marin*
in Finistère ports, starting at Le Guilvinec: warm rooms, a library and
classes in navigation, as an alternative to the taverns. Fifteen were built
by 1952 ([Wikipedia (fr): Abri du marin](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). More on
the fishing side in [Fishing through history](fishing-through-history.html).

## Lights and lifeboats

The rocks of western Brittany called for some of the hardest lighthouse work
anywhere. Ar-Men, at the end of the Chaussée de Sein, took 14 years to build
(1867-1881). A team of men from the Île de Sein drilled the rock, landing
only when the sea allowed: in the first season they managed
8 hours of work on the rock. It was lit in 1881, and its last keepers were
lifted off by helicopter on 10 April 1990
([Wikipedia (fr): Phare d'Ar-Men](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)). See
[Lighthouses](lighthouses.html).

Rescue had its own Breton society. The Hospitaliers sauveteurs bretons were
founded in Rennes in 1873; in 1967 they merged with the national society to
form the SNSM
([Wikipedia (fr): Hospitaliers-Sauveteurs bretons](../library/wikipedia-fr.html)).
See [Lifeboats and sea rescue](sea-rescue.html) and
[France: CROSS, SNSM and calling for help](../regions/france-search-and-rescue.html).
