Almost every fishing boat, workboat and cruising yacht today has a diesel
engine. That was not obvious in 1900, when sailing craft were only starting
to take engines at all. This page tells how it happened.

## The idea: heat from compression

A diesel squeezes air until it is hot enough to light the fuel sprayed into
it. Nothing else ignites the charge: no spark, no hot plate. That is why it is
also called a compression-ignition engine
([NAVPERS 16178, section 1-2](../library/navpers-16178-fundamentals-of-diesel-engines.html)).
For how it works in your boat, see [How a diesel engine works](../know-your-boat/how-a-diesel-engine-works.html).

Rudolf Diesel published his theory of a "rational heat motor" and took a
German patent in February 1893. His first prototype, built in Augsburg, fired
for the first time on 10 August 1893 and wrecked its indicator. After two more
prototypes and long tests, the engine was ready for public trials in
February 1897, and the first commercial engine went to work in a match factory
in 1898 ([Wikipedia: Diesel engine](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## The hot-bulb engine came first at sea

Small boats went another way first. Herbert Akroyd Stuart in England built a
hot-bulb oil engine from 1886, made under licence from 1891. Fuel is injected
into a bulb heated red-hot by a blow-lamp; the compression is much lower than a
diesel's, about 3:1 to 5:1 against 15:1 to 20:1
([Wikipedia: Hot-bulb engine](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). In 1914 a British
handbook put hot-bulb compression at 150 to 200 lb/in² (10 to 14 bar),
against about 450 lb/in² (31 bar) for a diesel. The hot-bulb engine was slow,
heavy and simple, running at 200 to 500 rpm, and burned cheap heavy oils. It
was used in barges, fishing boats, auxiliary schooners, tugs and coasters, and
was already very common in Scandinavian fishing craft. For a while it was
sold as the "semi-diesel"
([Marine Oil-Engine Handbook, ch. XI](../library/marine-oil-engine-handbook-1914.html)).

Sweden alone had over 70 makers; in the 1920s one Swedish maker held about 80%
of the world market. A Norwegian hot-bulb make was a favourite in small fishing
boats, and its last semi-diesel was built in 1969
([Wikipedia: Hot-bulb engine](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

## Why the true diesel was slow to reach small boats

The early diesel blew its fuel in with compressed air at about 900 lb/in²
(62 bar), so it needed an air compressor, and it was costly and delicate in
small sizes. In 1914 diesels were "practically never" built below 50 hp and
had made little headway in fishing vessels against hot-bulb and paraffin
motors, even though they used about 20% less fuel
([Marine Oil-Engine Handbook, ch. XVI](../library/marine-oil-engine-handbook-1914.html)).
In the same years, Scottish herring drifters such as the Zulu and Fifie types
were being fitted with motors for the first time
([Marine Oil-Engine Handbook, ch. XVIII](../library/marine-oil-engine-handbook-1914.html)).

For small craft, the engineer A. E. Seaton advised in 1918 that paraffin
engines were popular and convenient, and that "petrol, however, should be
avoided on shipboard except in very special cases"
([Seaton, p. 60](../library/seaton-manual-marine-engineering-1918.html)). The
fire risk of petrol is one reason the diesel won.

## Ships go diesel

| Year | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1903 | River tanker *Vandal* (Russia) and *Petite-Pierre* (France): first diesel vessels |
| 1904 | French submarine *Aigrette*: first diesel submarine |
| 1905 | First turbochargers for diesel engines (Büchi) |
| 1912 | *Selandia* (Denmark): first large ocean-going motor ship |
| 1955 | Turbocharging becomes standard on one major maker's two-stroke marine diesels |
| c. 1960 | Most new ships built with diesel engines |

Sources: [Wikipedia: Vandal (tanker), Diesel engine, MS Selandia, Marine propulsion](../library/wikipedia-en.html).

*Vandal* used three 120 hp diesels driving generators, with electric motors on
the propellers. *Selandia*, built by Burmeister & Wain in Copenhagen, left on
her maiden voyage to Bangkok on 22 February 1912. She had no funnel, and people
called her a "phantom ship" because she made no smoke. Within ten years,
over 2 million deadweight tons of shipping ran on diesels
([Wikipedia: Vandal; MS Selandia](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).

Seaton listed the advantages in 1918: a small consumption of a safe and cheap
fuel, long range, and no boiler-room crew at all. He put the whole-plant
efficiency of a marine diesel at about 34%, against about 20% for a modern
turbine and 17% for a quadruple-expansion steam engine
([Seaton, Appendix A](../library/seaton-manual-marine-engineering-1918.html)).

## Lighter, faster, everywhere

The US Navy's 1945 diesel textbook described the trend: higher speeds, lighter
materials, V and other compact layouts, then supercharging. Between August 1941
and June 1943 the number of diesel-driven US Navy ships grew thirteen-fold, and
the textbook listed four advantages: power for weight, reliability, low fuel
use and less fire risk than petrol
([NAVPERS 16178, sections 1-3 to 1-6](../library/navpers-16178-fundamentals-of-diesel-engines.html)).

Rescue boats followed the same path. The RNLI's first motor lifeboats used
petrol; after the First World War it changed to diesel because diesels used
less fuel and gave longer range
([Wikipedia: Royal National Lifeboat Institution](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
See [Lifeboats and sea rescue](sea-rescue.html).

Today large ships use slow two-stroke diesels, often under 120 rpm and coupled
straight to the propeller, or medium-speed four-strokes with a gearbox
([Wikipedia: Marine propulsion](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Small boats use
high-speed diesels, but the principle is Diesel's of 1893: clean air, high
compression and clean fuel. Most breakdowns still come from one of those three;
see [Engine turns over but won't start](../fix-it/engine-wont-start.html),
[Bleeding air from a diesel fuel system](../fix-it/bleeding-a-diesel.html) and
[Water or bug in the diesel](../fix-it/fuel-contamination.html).
