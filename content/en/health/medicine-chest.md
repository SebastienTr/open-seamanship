A medicine chest is not a pharmacy for treating yourself. On a boat its main
job is to let a doctor, talking to you by radio, treat a patient with what you
carry. The radio medical centres ask what medicine chest is on board as one of
their first questions, and they ask crews to call before giving any medicine
([Pub. 117, p. 5-4](../library/nga-pub-117-2014.html)). The US Public Health
Service guide makes the same point: for anything serious, the person caring for
a patient should work with a doctor ashore, and arrangements for that contact
should be made before they are needed
([PHS, p. xiv](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)). See
[Getting medical advice by radio](getting-medical-advice-by-radio.html).

## Plan it for your boat

The PHS guide says there is no single right list. Each vessel should draw up
its own, for each voyage, considering
([PHS, p. G-1](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)):

- the skills of whoever will give care;
- the size, age, sex and health of the crew, and any known conditions;
- the length of the voyage and the distance from land;
- how you can reach a doctor (VHF, satellite, phone);
- how quickly you could reach medical care ashore;
- the risks of the work (fishing injuries, diving and so on);
- passengers, and their number, age and health.

Ask each crew member before sailing about their illnesses, allergies and
regular medicines, the same questions a doctor will ask after an accident
([PHS, p. 1-3](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)), and make sure they
bring enough of their own medicines for the whole trip and some spare.

The table groups the kinds of items found in the PHS list. It is a planning
aid, not a shopping list; the quantities and the choice of medicine must be
agreed with a doctor or pharmacist.

The PHS guide also calls for a low-reading thermometer, blankets and
hot-water bottles on board wherever there is a risk of hypothermia
([PHS, p. 1-19](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).

## Keep it in date and ready

- **Rotate stock.** Medicines have expiry dates. Keep sterile items sealed and
  use or replace everything before it expires
  ([PHS, pp. G-1 and G-15](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- **Check equipment regularly.** Broken equipment cannot be fixed during an
  emergency ([PHS, p. G-1](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
  Oxygen cylinders must never touch oil or grease and should be stored
  upright and secured ([PHS, p. G-8](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- **Lock up** strong painkillers and other controlled drugs, as the law
  requires ([PHS, p. G-15](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- **Keep a written list** of contents, with strengths and expiry dates, next
  to the radio as well as in the chest.
- **Keep a log** of every treatment and every medicine given: what, how much,
  when, by whom, and any reaction
  ([PHS, pp. 1-40 to 1-41](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- **Train.** The person using the kit should know how to use it
  ([PHS, p. G-1](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)). A first-aid
  course with a marine focus is worth more than any extra item.

## The liferaft's kit

A liferaft carries its own small first-aid kit. The PHS guide lists one with
dressings, scissors, forceps, a resuscitation instruction card, seasickness
tablets and sun protection, and warns not to open sealed rafts to inspect
them ([PHS, p. G-9](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)). See
[Surviving in a liferaft](survival-in-a-liferaft.html) and
[Liferafts](../run-the-boat/liferafts.html).

## Old lists and old advice

Medicine changes. The 1950 army lifeboat kit contained ammonia inhalants,
benzedrine and phenobarbital tablets, and the same manual told survivors to
give "stimulants" to the shocked
([FM 21-22, pp. 93 to 96](../library/fm-21-22-survival-at-sea-1950.html)).
None of this is used today. Even the 2003 PHS list includes syrup of ipecac,
once used to make poisoned people vomit, which is no longer advised, and
silver sulfadiazine burn cream, which is no longer recommended for routine
burns ([PHS, pp. G-14 to G-17](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html);
[Wikipedia, 'Syrup of ipecac'](../library/wikipedia-en.html);
[Wikipedia, 'Burn'](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). Throw old ipecac away. For seasickness, the
choice of medicines has also moved on
([Wikipedia, 'Motion sickness'](../library/wikipedia-en.html)). If your kit
list comes from an old book, ask a doctor or pharmacist to review it.

## Using medicines from the chest

- Ask about allergies before giving anything
  ([PHS, p. 1-24](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- Follow the doctor's instructions or the packet; never give more.
- Give nothing by mouth to anyone who is unconscious, drowsy, or may need an
  operation, unless the doctor agrees.
- After a head injury, do not give painkillers that cause drowsiness without
  a doctor's advice ([PHS, p. 1-24](../library/phs-ships-medicine-chest.html)).
- Write down what you gave and when.
