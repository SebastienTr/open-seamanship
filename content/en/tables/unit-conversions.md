The nautical mile, the knot and the fathom are the sea's own units. Since
1929 the international nautical mile is exactly 1852 metres, and the knot is
one nautical mile per hour. Everything else on this page follows from that
and from the 1959 definition of the foot (0.3048 m exactly)
([Bowditch vol. 2, Appendix C](../library/bowditch.html)).

Values marked **exact** are definitions. Others are rounded; more digits
rarely matter at sea.

Rule of thumb: 1 nautical mile is very nearly 1 minute of latitude. That is
why you measure distance on the latitude scale at the side of a chart, never on
the longitude scale ([Nautical mile, wikipedia-en](../library/wikipedia-en.html)).
See [Reading a chart](../run-the-boat/reading-a-chart.html).

Charts may give depths in metres, in feet, or in fathoms and feet. The unit is
printed in large letters at the top and bottom of the chart ("SOUNDINGS IN
FATHOMS" and so on). Check it before you trust a number
([Bowditch vol. 1, section 534](../library/bowditch.html)).

Barometers on board read in hectopascals (= millibars) or inches of mercury.
Oil and tyre gauges read in bar or psi.

## Temperature

- °F = °C × 9/5 + 32
- °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9
- Water freezes at 0 °C (32 °F) and boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level
  ([Bowditch vol. 2, Table 29](../library/bowditch.html)).

| °C | °F |
| --- | --- |
| −10 | 14 |
| 0 | 32 |
| 10 | 50 |
| 20 | 68 |
| 30 | 86 |
| 37 | 98.6 |
| 80 | 176 |
| 90 | 194 |
| 100 | 212 |

For engine temperatures, use your engine's own figures; see
[Engine overheating](../fix-it/engine-overheating.html).
